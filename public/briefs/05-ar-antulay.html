A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak

The seven judges who recalled the Supreme Court's own direction — and, in doing so, wrote the law on when a judgment of the highest court is a nullity.

(1988) 2 SCC 602AIR 1988 SC 1531[1988] Supp 1 SCR 129 April 19887 Judges

Before you read — two things about this brief

The date. You wrote 29.04.1998. The judgment is 29 April 1988 — same day and month, ten years out. There is no Antulay v. Nayak decision of 1998. Everything below is the 1988 seven-Judge judgment.

The source. No judgment file was uploaded to this session, so this is built from the text of the judgment as published, the Supreme Court's own AOR leading-case headnote, and authoritative reports — not from a file you supplied. Every proposition carries a confidence tag, and §30 sets out exactly what I could and could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Facts. Antulay, former Chief Minister of Maharashtra, faced a private corruption complaint before a Special Judge.
  2. The error. On 16 February 1984 a five-Judge Constitution Bench, while setting aside his discharge, also withdrew the cases from the Special Judge and transferred them to a judge of the Bombay High Court for a speedy trial — a direction nobody had asked for and nobody was heard on.
  3. Why that was fatal. Section 7(1), Criminal Law Amendment Act 1952 makes trial by a Special Judge a condition sine qua non. Jurisdiction is created by legislation, not by judicial order — not even the Supreme Court's.
  4. Four rights lost. Trial by the Special Judge; revision to the High Court; a first appeal to the High Court; and a further appeal to the Supreme Court. Antulay was singled out for a procedure the law did not provide — Article 14 — and tried otherwise than by the procedure established by law — Article 21.
  5. The rule. The direction was per incuriam — given in ignorance of a mandatory statutory provision and of binding authority — and therefore a nullity. The Court has inherent power to recall such an order ex debito justitiae, without a formal review petition.
  6. The maxim. Actus curiae neminem gravabit — an act of the Court shall prejudice no one.
  7. Held. Appeal allowed. All proceedings after 16 February 1984, including 79 charges framed by the High Court judge, quashed. Trial remitted to the Special Judge.
  8. Why it matters to you. This is the foundation of the Supreme Court's power over its own final orders — the doctrinal parent of the curative petition in Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002), which is itself Leading Case No. 15 on your list.

03One-Line Ratio

Precise, and not simplified past the point of accuracy

One-line ratio

A direction of the Supreme Court which confers on a court a jurisdiction that no statute confers, given in ignorance of a mandatory statutory provision and without hearing the party affected, is per incuriam and a nullity; it violates Articles 14 and 21; and the Court possesses inherent power to recall it ex debito justitiae, without the formalities of a review petition, because no act of the Court may prejudice a litigant.

Exam memory hook

"The Court that erred is the Court that cures." — actus curiae neminem gravabit.

04Facts

Material facts separated from background

Background facts

  • A.R. Antulay was Chief Minister of Maharashtra and resigned in January 1982 following allegations concerning sugar co-operatives and trusts.
  • R.S. Nayak, a private complainant, prosecuted him. 🟢 CERTAIN
  • The offences alleged were under ss. 161 and 165 IPC and s. 5 of the Prevention of Corruption Act 1947.

Material facts — the ones the ratio turns on

  1. The complaint was filed before a Special Judge, the only forum competent under s. 7(1) of the 1952 Act. 🟢 CERTAIN
  2. On 25 July 1983 the Special Judge discharged Antulay, holding that as an MLA he was a public servant and sanction was required. 🟢 CERTAIN
  3. On 16 February 1984 a five-Judge Constitution Bench set aside that discharge — and, going further, withdrew the two Special Cases from the Special Judge and transferred them to the Bombay High Court, requesting assignment to a sitting High Court judge for a day-to-day trial. 🟢 CERTAIN
  4. Nobody had asked for that transfer, and Antulay was not heard on it. The Court itself later described the direction as given without argument or submission on the point. 🟢 CERTAIN
  5. A High Court judge then tried the case and on 24 July 1986 framed 79 charges. 🟢 CERTAIN

The fact that decides the case

Fact 4. Everything else follows from it. A direction reached without adversarial argument, on a point nobody raised, is the classic setting for per incuriam — and it is also, on its own, a breach of audi alteram partem.

05Procedural History

For this case the procedural history is the case

Jurisdiction invoked, stage by stage

  • Article 136 — the appellate route by which the corruption proceedings reached the Supreme Court in 1984 and again thereafter. 🟢
  • Article 32 — invoked on 17.4.1984 to attack the transfer as a nullity, and dismissed. This matters: a judicial order of the Supreme Court is not amenable to Article 32 (the Mirajkar position). The relief had to come from within the proceedings, not from a fresh writ. 🟢
  • Article 137 — review. The 1984 order had noted that a review petition lay. The seven Judges held the Court was not confined to that route where the order was a nullity. 🟢
  • Article 142 — the source relied on for the 1984 direction. Held: Article 142 cannot be used to achieve indirectly what a statute forbids. 🟡 STRONG
  • Article 139A — transfer of cases; considered and found not to authorise what was done. 🟡
  • CrPC s. 407 — the High Court's transfer power; held subject to the overriding mandate of s. 7(1) of the 1952 Act, so it does not permit the High Court to withdraw a case to itself from a Special Judge. 🟢

Procedural question squarely before the seven Judges: may a larger Bench recall an operative direction of a smaller Constitution Bench, inter partes, otherwise than by review under Article 137?

06Issues

Kept separate by category — do not merge them

Jurisdictional

Issue 1. Does s. 7(1) of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1952 confer exclusive jurisdiction on the Special Judge, such that no court may transfer the trial elsewhere?
Provision: s. 7(1), 1952 Act; CrPC s. 407. Answer: Yes — it is a condition sine qua non. Importance: the foundation of everything else. 🟢

Issue 2. Can a court — including the Supreme Court — create or enlarge jurisdiction?
Answer: No. Conferment of jurisdiction is a legislative function. 🟢

Constitutional

Issue 3. Did the transfer violate Article 14?
Answer: Yes — the appellant was singled out for a procedure not provided by law, without any rational basis, contrary to Anwar Ali Sarkar. 🟢

Issue 4. Did it violate Article 21?
Answer: Yes — the 1952 Act was the only procedure established by law, and he was tried outside it. 🟢

Issue 5. Can Article 142 sustain a direction that contradicts an express statutory provision?
Answer: No. 🟡 STRONG

Procedural / on the Court's own powers

Issue 6. Were the 1984 directions per incuriam?
Answer: Yes — given in ignorance of s. 7(1) and of Anwar Ali Sarkar, and without argument on the point. 🟢

Issue 7. Is such a direction a nullity, and may it be ignored or must it be set aside?
Answer: It is a nullity and the Court will recall it. 🟢

Issue 8. May the Court correct itself without a review petition under Article 137?
Answer: Yes — in exercise of inherent jurisdiction, ex debito justitiae, where fundamental rights have been taken away by the Court's own order. 🟢

Issue 9. May a larger Bench undo the operative direction of a smaller Bench inter partes?
Answer: Yes, on jurisdictional and constitutional grounds — but this is precisely where the Bench divided. 🟡

Natural justice

Issue 10. Does a direction made without hearing the affected party offend audi alteram partem even when made by the Supreme Court?
Answer: Yes. 🟢

08Ratio Decidendi

Five propositions, each with rule, reasoning, application and conclusion

Proposition 1 — Exclusive statutory jurisdiction cannot be displaced by judicial order

Rule. Where a statute vests trial of an offence exclusively in a designated court, that vesting is a condition sine qua non and no court may transfer the trial elsewhere. Reasoning. The creation and enlargement of jurisdiction is legislative in character; no court, superior or inferior, can enlarge its own or another's jurisdiction. Application. Section 7(1) vested the trial in the Special Judge; the direction moved it to a High Court judge. Conclusion. The direction was beyond power. 🟢 CERTAIN

Proposition 2 — A direction given in ignorance of a mandatory provision is per incuriam

Rule. A decision given in ignorance or forgetfulness of an inconsistent statutory provision or of binding authority is per incuriam and lacks precedential and, where it operates against fundamental rights, operative force. Reasoning. The 1984 Bench was not addressed on s. 7(1) at all; there was no argument, no submission and no decision on the point. Application. The Bench was also unaware of Anwar Ali Sarkar. Conclusion. The direction was per incuriam. 🟢 CERTAIN

Proposition 3 — Articles 14 and 21 bind the Court's own directions

Rule. A direction singling out one accused for a procedure the law does not provide offends Article 14; trying him otherwise than under the applicable statute offends Article 21. Reasoning. The guarantee runs against State action, and a judicial direction that deprives a citizen of a statutory forum and of appellate remedies is not immune. Application. Four rights were lost — Special Judge trial, s. 9 revision, first appeal, further appeal. Conclusion. Both Articles violated. 🟢 CERTAIN

Proposition 4 — Inherent power to recall, ex debito justitiae

Rule. The Supreme Court is not powerless to correct an error of its own which has deprived a citizen of fundamental rights; it may do so in exercise of inherent jurisdiction in any proceeding pending before it, without insisting on the formalities of a review application. Reasoning. Actus curiae neminem gravabit — an act of the Court shall prejudice no one — is founded on justice and good sense. Application. The appeal against the framing of charges was a pending proceeding in which the relief could be given. Conclusion. The direction was recalled. 🟢 CERTAIN

Proposition 5 — Article 142 does not override statute

Rule. The power to do complete justice cannot be exercised to achieve indirectly what an express statutory provision forbids. Reasoning. Complete justice is done within, not against, the law. Application. Neither Article 142 nor Article 139A could authorise a transfer that s. 7(1) prohibited. Conclusion. No constitutional source sustained the direction. 🟡 STRONG — clearly supported by the reasoning; expressed most directly by Ranganathan, J.

Do not overclaim

It is tempting to write that Antulay holds "the Supreme Court can always recall any of its orders." It does not. The holding is tied to an order that was a nullity — beyond jurisdiction, per incuriam, and destructive of fundamental rights — and the Court stressed the exceptional character of the power.

10Judicial Reasoning

Provision → precedent → interpretation → application → conclusion

The competing positions the Court had to resolve

Argument A (respondent / the minority's instinct): the 1984 direction was a judicial order of a Constitution Bench, inter partes, and had attained finality. If it was wrong, the remedy was review under Article 137 before the same Judges. A larger Bench cannot simply undo the operative directions of a smaller one; finality is itself a constitutional value.

Argument B (the majority): finality presupposes jurisdiction. An order made without jurisdiction, in ignorance of a mandatory statute, without hearing the party, and which strips him of fundamental rights, is a nullity — and a nullity does not acquire finality merely because time has passed. To insist on the forms of review would be to let the Court's own mistake work an injustice it has the power to undo.

Resolution: Argument B prevailed. The Court held that where its own order has deprived a citizen of fundamental rights, it will act ex debito justitiae — as a matter of right owed to the litigant, not of discretion — without the formalities of a review application.

12Precedent Chain

Everything after 1988 is flagged as a post-judgment development

Post-judgment development — state it as such

Rupa Ashok Hurra v. Ashok Hurra, (2002) 4 SCC 388 (10 April 2002, five Judges) created the curative petition: after a review has been dismissed, a final judgment may still be reconsidered to prevent abuse of process or a gross miscarriage of justice, on limited grounds — a breach of natural justice where the party was not served or not heard, or where a Judge failed to disclose a connection raising an apprehension of bias. It must be certified by a senior advocate and is circulated to the three senior-most Judges and the Judges who passed the judgment, ordinarily without oral hearing. 🟢 CERTAIN

M/s Brahmaputra Concrete Pipe Industries v. Assam State Electricity Board, 2024 INSC 145 (26 February 2024) holds that whether a curative petition is maintainable is a judicial determination for a Bench — the Registry cannot refuse registration — and confirms that curative jurisdiction flows from Articles 129 and 142, not from the review power. 🟢 CERTAIN

13Dissent & Concurring Opinions

Where the seven Judges actually divided — read this carefully

Venkatachaliah, J. — dissenting

  • The 1984 decision was an inter partes adjudication that had attained finality; a larger Bench cannot undo the operative finality of a smaller Bench's judgment between the same parties.
  • If it was wrong, the constitutional route is review under Article 137, before the same Judges.
  • Actus curiae neminem gravabit addresses the court's procedural errors, not its considered judicial conclusions.
  • There was no patent lack of jurisdiction; the invocation of Anwar Ali Sarkar was out of place.

Ranganathan, J. — concurring in part, dissenting in part

  • Agreed that s. 7(1) created an exclusive jurisdiction which barred the transfer, and that Article 142 could not achieve indirectly what the statute forbade.
  • Disagreed on relief: a substantial trial had already been conducted and evidence recorded; the appellant had delayed; and the power to recall ex debito justitiae is exceptional, for the rarest of rare cases.

The critical difference in one line

The majority asked "was there jurisdiction?" The dissent asked "is there finality?" Everything else follows from which question you put first.

14Final Holding

In precise legal language

Held

  1. Section 7(1) of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1952 vests the trial of the specified offences exclusively in a Special Judge, and that vesting is a condition sine qua non.
  2. No court, superior or inferior, can create or enlarge jurisdiction; conferment of jurisdiction is a legislative function.
  3. The directions of 16 February 1984 withdrawing the cases from the Special Judge and transferring them to the High Court were made in ignorance of s. 7(1) and of Anwar Ali Sarkar, without argument and without hearing the appellant, and were therefore per incuriam and a nullity.
  4. Those directions deprived the appellant of rights under Articles 14 and 21 and offended audi alteram partem.
  5. The Supreme Court is not powerless to correct an error of its own that deprives a citizen of fundamental rights, and may do so ex debito justitiae in exercise of inherent jurisdiction in a pending proceeding, without the formalities of a review application.
  6. Section 407 CrPC is subject to the overriding mandate of s. 7(1) and does not permit the High Court to withdraw such a case to itself.

Result

Appeal allowed by majority. The directions of 16 February 1984 are recalled and set aside. All proceedings taken pursuant to them — including the framing of 79 charges by the High Court judge — are set aside and quashed. The trial is to proceed in accordance with law, that is, under the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1952, before a competent Special Judge.

16AOR-Specific Importance

Advocate-on-Record mode — this is the richest case on your list for Paper I

Points of AOR practice this case supplies

  • Jurisdiction cannot be conferred by consent, waiver or judicial order. If the forum is wrong, no amount of participation cures it — a point that recurs in maintainability questions.
  • Recall is not review. Review is a statutory remedy on limited grounds before the same Bench; recall of a nullity is an exercise of inherent power. Do not describe Antulay as a review case.
  • Article 142 has limits. It cannot be used to override express statutory provisions or fundamental rights (Prem Chand Garg, applied). A frequent essay question.
  • Bench strength and precedent. A larger Bench may reconsider a smaller Bench's decision. Relevant to reference practice and to how you frame a submission that a judgment is per incuriam.
  • Article 32 is not a route against judicial orders of the Supreme Court. Know why the 1984 writ failed.
  • Natural justice binds the Court's own orders. A direction affecting a party made without hearing him is vulnerable — useful whenever you are attacking an order made suo motu.
  • Drafting leverage. The grounds this case supplies for an application to recall: want of inherent jurisdiction; order per incuriam; made without notice or hearing; deprivation of a statutory forum and of appellate remedies; violation of Arts. 14 and 21.
  • Curative practice. Certification by a senior advocate, circulation to the three senior-most Judges and the Judges concerned, ordinarily no oral hearing — and, since 2024, the Registry cannot refuse registration.

185-Mark Questions

Model answer, essential points, and the mistake most candidates make

Q1Explain the doctrine of per incuriam as applied in Antulay.

Model answer. A decision is per incuriam where it is given in ignorance or forgetfulness of an inconsistent statutory provision or of binding authority. In Antulay, the five-Judge Bench directed transfer of the trial without being addressed on s. 7(1) of the 1952 Act, which vests exclusive jurisdiction in a Special Judge, and without reference to Anwar Ali Sarkar. There was no argument, no submission and no decision on the point. The seven-Judge Bench accordingly held the direction per incuriam and a nullity, and recalled it.

Essential points: definition; the two things overlooked (statute and precedent); the absence of argument; the consequence — nullity, not merely error.

Common mistake: treating per incuriam as meaning "wrongly decided". It is not about the merits; it is about what the court was not told.

Q2Distinguish review under Article 137 from recall ex debito justitiae.

Model answer. Review is a remedy conferred by Article 137 read with the Supreme Court Rules; it lies before the same Bench, on limited grounds such as an error apparent on the face of the record, by petition and within the prescribed time. Recall ex debito justitiae is not a statutory remedy but an exercise of inherent jurisdiction, available where the order is a nullity — made without jurisdiction, per incuriam, or without hearing the affected party — and where it has deprived a citizen of fundamental rights. Antulay holds the Court is not confined to the review route in such a case and may act in any pending proceeding without the formalities of a review application.

Essential points: source (Article 137 vs inherent power); forum; grounds; procedure; when each is available.

Common mistake: calling Antulay a review judgment. It is expressly not.

Q3Why did the transfer violate Articles 14 and 21?

Model answer. Article 14: the appellant alone was subjected to a procedure the statute provided for nobody — a special procedure without rational basis, the vice condemned in Anwar Ali Sarkar. Article 21: the 1952 Act was the only procedure established by law for trying these offences, and trying him before a High Court judge was a deprivation of liberty otherwise than by that procedure. Concretely, he lost four rights: trial by the Special Judge, revision under s. 9, a first appeal, and a further appeal.

Essential points: both Articles, separately reasoned; the four lost rights; Anwar Ali Sarkar.

Common mistake: arguing only Article 21 and forgetting the singling-out point under Article 14.

Q4Can Article 142 be used to transfer a case contrary to statute?

Model answer. No. Following Prem Chand Garg, an order under Article 142 cannot be inconsistent with fundamental rights or with express statutory provisions. What the statute forbids directly cannot be achieved indirectly under Article 142. Section 7(1) barred the transfer; neither Article 142 nor Article 139A could supply the jurisdiction. Complete justice is done within the law, not against it.

Essential points: Prem Chand Garg; the direct/indirect formulation; the interaction with s. 7(1).

Common mistake: presenting Article 142 as an unlimited reservoir of power.

Q5Summarise the dissent.

Model answer. Venkatachaliah, J. held that the 1984 decision was an inter partes adjudication that had attained finality, and that a larger Bench cannot undo the operative directions of a smaller one; the constitutional route was review under Article 137 before the same Judges. He confined actus curiae neminem gravabit to procedural errors rather than considered judicial conclusions, and found no patent lack of jurisdiction. Ranganathan, J. agreed that s. 7(1) barred the transfer but declined the relief, given that a substantial trial had been held, the appellant had delayed, and the power to recall is exceptional.

Essential points: finality; Article 137 as the proper route; the narrowing of the maxim; Ranganathan J.'s split position.

Common mistake: treating Ranganathan, J. as a straightforward dissenter. He agreed on jurisdiction and disagreed only on relief.

19Long-Answer Questions

What the examiner is testing, and how to structure it

L1"An order of the Supreme Court can never be a nullity." Discuss with reference to A.R. Antulay.

Testing: whether you can hold finality and legality in tension rather than reciting one side.

Structure: the value of finality → the counter-principle that jurisdiction precedes finality → Antulay's facts → the three vitiating features (no jurisdiction, per incuriam, no hearing) → the fundamental-rights dimension → the dissent's answer → the settlement in Rupa Ashok Hurra → conclusion that the proposition in the question is too absolute.

Must appear: s. 7(1); per incuriam; ex debito justitiae; Arts. 14 and 21; the maxim; Venkatachaliah J.'s dissent.

Extra marks: noting that Antulay was decided in a pending proceeding, not by a free-standing application, and that Hurra later supplied the missing procedure.

Trap: writing as though Antulay licences reopening any order.

L2Trace the evolution of the Supreme Court's power to correct its own final orders.

Testing: the precedent chain, and whether you keep 1988 and 2002 apart.

Structure: Article 137 and Order XLVII → Prem Chand Garg on the limits of Art. 142 → Mirajkar closing off Art. 32 → Antulay (1988) opening inherent recall for nullities → Rupa Ashok Hurra (2002) creating the curative petition and its conditions → Brahmaputra Concrete (2024) on registration and on Arts. 129 and 142 as the source → conclusion on the balance struck.

Must appear: the three routes; the Art. 32 bar; the grounds for a curative petition; senior-advocate certification.

Extra marks: the observation that curative jurisdiction is not derived from the review power.

Trap: saying Antulay created the curative petition. It did not — it created the conceptual space for it.

L3"Jurisdiction is conferred by law, not by courts." Examine.

Testing: separation of powers applied to a very concrete problem.

Structure: the proposition → why conferment is legislative → s. 7(1) as a condition sine qua non → CrPC s. 407 read subject to it → why even the Supreme Court cannot enlarge jurisdiction → Art. 142 and Art. 139A do not help → consequences: consent and waiver cannot cure → conclusion.

Must appear: the sine qua non formulation; the direct/indirect point on Art. 142.

Extra marks: noting the 1984 Bench's motive was expedition, and that a benign purpose cannot supply jurisdiction.

L4Do the principles of natural justice bind the Supreme Court's own orders?

Testing: whether you can apply audi alteram partem upward.

Structure: the rule → its application to courts as well as administrators → the 1984 direction made without argument or notice → the Court's own acknowledgement → the remedy that followed → the limits (Ranganathan J.'s point that notice and opportunity are not invariably absent) → link forward to Hurra, where breach of natural justice is a curative ground.

Must appear: that the direction was suo motu and unargued; that this alone was a vitiating feature.

Trap: confusing the absence of a hearing on the transfer with an absence of hearing in the appeal generally.

M1The bench in A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak (1988) comprised:

  3. Nine Judges
  4. Three Judges

Answer: B. Seven. A is the strength of the Bench that gave the 1984 direction — the commonest confusion. C and D are simply wrong.

  1. Correct but inexpedient
  2. An error apparent on the face of the record, curable only by review
  3. Per incuriam and a nullity
  4. Voidable at the appellant's option

Answer: C. B is the dissent's position, not the holding. D imports a private-law concept the judgment does not use.

M3Which provision made trial by a Special Judge mandatory?
  1. Section 6, Criminal Law Amendment Act 1952
  2. Section 7(1), Criminal Law Amendment Act 1952
  3. Section 407, CrPC 1973
  4. Section 5, Prevention of Corruption Act 1947

Answer: B. A specifies the offences; B allocates them exclusively to Special Judges. C is the transfer power held subject to B. D creates the offence.

M4The Court's power to recall was exercised:
  1. On a review petition under Article 137
  2. On a fresh writ petition under Article 32
  3. On a curative petition
  4. In exercise of inherent jurisdiction in a pending proceeding, without a review petition

Answer: D. B was tried in 1984 and failed. C did not exist until 2002.

M5Which case did the 1984 Bench overlook?
  1. Prem Chand Garg
  2. Anwar Ali Sarkar
  3. Mirajkar
  4. Bengal Immunity

Answer: B. The others were discussed in 1988 but the per incuriam finding rests on ignorance of s. 7(1) and of Anwar Ali Sarkar.

M6Which judge concurred in part and dissented in part?
  1. Venkatachaliah, J.
  2. B.C. Ray, J.
  3. Ranganathan, J.
  4. Ranganath Misra, J.

Answer: C. A dissented outright. B and D concurred. Note the near-identical names in D and C — a real trap in a hurried reading.

M7After the recall, the trial was to be held before:
  1. A judge of the Bombay High Court
  2. A Special Judge under the 1952 Act
  3. A Sessions Judge
  4. A Chief Judicial Magistrate

Answer: B.

M8The maxim actus curiae neminem gravabit means:
  1. No one may be a judge in his own cause
  2. An act of the Court shall prejudice no one
  3. Hear the other side
  4. Where there is a right there is a remedy

Answer: B. A is nemo judex; C is audi alteram partem; D is ubi jus ibi remedium.

M9Which is NOT a route to correct a final Supreme Court order?
  1. Review under Article 137
  2. Curative petition
  3. Recall of a nullity in a pending proceeding
  4. Writ petition under Article 32

Answer: D. Article 32 does not lie against a judicial order of the Supreme Court — the Mirajkar position, and the reason Antulay's 1984 writ failed.

M10Which of the following did the 1988 judgment NOT disturb?
  1. The transfer of the cases to the High Court
  2. The 79 charges framed on 24.7.1986
  3. The holding that an MLA is not a public servant
  4. The proceedings held after 16.2.1984

Answer: C. Only the transfer direction and what followed from it were undone. The public-servant holding survived — a point that separates careful candidates from careless ones.

25Memory Architecture

Built for recall under time pressure

Five things I must remember

  1. Seven Judges undid five. 1988 recalls 1984.
  2. s. 7(1) — Special Judge only. Jurisdiction comes from the legislature.
  3. Per incuriam → nullity → recall. The three-step engine of the case.
  4. Four rights lost → Articles 14 and 21.
  5. Actus curiae neminem gravabit.

Three things I must not forget

  1. It was not a review, and Article 32 was not the route.
  2. Only the transfer direction was recalled — the public-servant holding survived.
  3. Ranganathan, J. is not a full dissenter. Venkatachaliah, J. is.

One story that links it all

A Chief Minister is prosecuted before the one court Parliament designated — the Special Judge. He gets discharged. The Supreme Court, five strong and impatient for a quick trial, sets the discharge aside and — without anyone asking — lifts the case out of that court and hands it to a High Court judge. Nobody notices s. 7(1). Four years and seventy-nine charges later, seven Judges look at what happened and see a man who lost his trial court, his revision, his first appeal and his second appeal, all by an order made in ignorance and without a hearing. They call it what it is — per incuriam, a nullity — and they undo it, saying the Court's own act must prejudice no one. Fourteen years on, that instinct hardens into the curative petition.

Facts → Issue → Provision → Precedent → Ratio → Result, in one paragraph.

26Flashcards

Twenty cards — retrieval, not trivia

F1Three-step engine of the case?

Per incuriam → nullity → recall ex debito justitiae.

F2What makes s. 7(1) special?

It vests trial exclusively in a Special Judge — a condition sine qua non, not a preference.

F3Who can create jurisdiction?

The legislature only. No court, superior or inferior, can create or enlarge it.

F4The four rights lost?

Special Judge trial · s. 9 revision · first appeal to the High Court · further appeal to the Supreme Court.

F5Why Article 14?

Singled out for a special procedure the law provides to nobody, without rational basis — Anwar Ali Sarkar.

F6Why Article 21?

The 1952 Act was the only procedure established by law; he was tried outside it.

F7Definition of per incuriam?

Given in ignorance or forgetfulness of an inconsistent statutory provision or of binding authority.

F8Was a review petition necessary?

No — the Court acted in inherent jurisdiction in a pending proceeding, without the formalities of review.

F9Does Article 32 lie against a Supreme Court judicial order?

No — Mirajkar. Antulay's 1984 writ was dismissed on that footing.

F10Limit on Article 142?

It cannot override express statutory provisions or fundamental rights — Prem Chand Garg; and cannot achieve indirectly what a statute forbids.

F11Effect on CrPC s. 407?

Read subject to s. 7(1); the High Court cannot withdraw such a case to itself from a Special Judge.

F12Who dissented, and on what?

Venkatachaliah, J. — on finality, and that review under Art. 137 was the proper route.

F13Ranganathan J.'s position?

Agreed s. 7(1) barred the transfer; disagreed on relief — trial substantially held, delay, and the power is for the rarest of rare cases.

F14What survived from the 1984 judgment?

Everything except the transfer directions — including that an MLA is not a public servant.

F15Which precedent was overlooked in 1984?

Anwar Ali Sarkar, AIR 1952 SC 75.

F16Which precedent licensed reconsideration?

Bengal Immunity — the Supreme Court is not bound by its own prior decisions.

F17The maxim and its translation?

Actus curiae neminem gravabit — an act of the Court shall prejudice no one.

F18Curative petition — grounds?

Breach of natural justice (not served or not heard), or a Judge's undisclosed connection raising apprehension of bias.

F19Curative petition — procedure?

Only after review dismissed; certified by a senior advocate; circulated to the three senior-most Judges and the Judges who passed the judgment; ordinarily no oral hearing.

F20Source of curative jurisdiction?

Articles 129 and 142 — not the power of review under Article 137 (Brahmaputra Concrete, 2024).

27Last-Night Revision Card

The section to read walking in — nothing else

A.R. Antulay v. R.S. Nayak, (1988) 2 SCC 602 — 29 April 1988 — 7 Judges

Facts. Antulay, ex-Chief Minister of Maharashtra, was prosecuted for corruption before a Special Judge. On 16.2.1984 a five-Judge Bench set aside his discharge and — unasked, unargued — withdrew the cases from the Special Judge and transferred them to a Bombay High Court judge, who framed 79 charges.

Issues. Is the Special Judge's jurisdiction exclusive? Was the direction per incuriam and a nullity? Can the Supreme Court recall its own order without a review petition?

Provisions. s. 7(1), Criminal Law Amendment Act 1952 (Special Judge only); s. 9 (revision); CrPC s. 407; Arts. 14, 21, 32, 136, 137, 139A, 142.

Ratio. A direction conferring jurisdiction no statute confers, given in ignorance of a mandatory provision and without hearing the party, is per incuriam and a nullity; it violates Arts. 14 and 21; and the Court may recall it ex debito justitiae in inherent jurisdiction, without the formalities of review — actus curiae neminem gravabit.

Key precedent. Anwar Ali Sarkar (Art. 14, special procedure) — the case the 1984 Bench overlooked. Prem Chand Garg — Art. 142 cannot override statute or fundamental rights.

Procedural point. Recall ≠ review. Article 32 does not lie against a judicial order of the Supreme Court (Mirajkar) — which is why the 1984 writ failed.

Holding. Appeal allowed. Directions of 16.2.1984 recalled; all subsequent proceedings including the 79 charges quashed; trial remitted to a Special Judge.

Exception / limit. The power is exceptional and tied to a nullity affecting fundamental rights — not a general licence to reopen final orders. Venkatachaliah, J. dissented on finality; Ranganathan, J. agreed on jurisdiction but not on relief.

Trap. Do not say five Judges; do not call it a review; do not say the whole 1984 judgment fell; do not attribute the curative petition to this case.

One-line takeaway. The Court that erred is the Court that cures — but only where its own order was a nullity.
