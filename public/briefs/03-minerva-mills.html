AOR Paper IVLeading Case 3 of 86Master Brief

Minerva Mills Ltd. v. Union of India

Parliament tried to write itself an unlimited amending power and to place every Directive Principle above Articles 14 and 19. The Court answered that a limited power cannot be used to make itself unlimited.

(1980) 3 SCC 625AIR 1980 SC 1789[1981] 1 SCR 20631 July 19805 Judges · 4:1 on Art. 31C

Read this first — two provisions, two different votes

Section 55 of the 42nd Amendment (Article 368(4) and (5)) was struck down unanimously, 5:0. Section 4 (the widened Article 31C) was struck down 4:1, Bhagwati J. dissenting. An answer that says "4:1" for the whole case is wrong on half of it.

Current position on Article 31C. The widening failed here. In 2024 a nine-judge Bench (Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra) held that Article 31C as upheld in Kesavananda — protecting laws under Article 39(b) and (c) — continues in force. Strong Keep the two versions apart; §12 does this.

On sources. No judgment file was uploaded. I worked from the Supreme Court's head-notes volume and the settled reported position. §30 lists what I could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Trigger. Minerva Mills, a textile company, had its management taken over (1971) and its undertaking nationalised under the Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, 1974.
  2. Shield. The Act sat in the Ninth Schedule (39th Amendment) and behind Article 31C; any challenge to those shields was barred by the new Article 368(4) and (5).
  3. Challenge. So the petitioners attacked the shields: sections 4 and 55 of the 42nd Amendment, 1976.
  4. Issue. Can Parliament remove all limits on its amending power, bar judicial review of amendments, and subordinate Articles 14 and 19 to every Directive Principle?
  5. Ratio. Limited amending power is itself a basic feature. So are judicial review and the harmony and balance between Parts III and IV.
  6. Holding. Section 55 void (5:0). Section 4 void (4:1).
  7. Significance. Kesavananda said there is a limit; Minerva Mills said Parliament cannot amend the limit away.

03One-Line Ratio

The sentence to reproduce

The ratio in one line

Parliament's power to amend is limited; that limitation is itself a basic feature, so Parliament cannot, by amendment, enlarge its own power into an absolute one, exclude judicial review, or destroy the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles. Certain

04Facts

What actually happened

  • 1970. The Central Government appointed a committee under section 15 of the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act, 1951 to investigate the affairs of Minerva Mills. Certain
  • January 1971. The committee reported.
  • 19 October 1971. An order under section 18A authorised the National Textile Corporation to take over the management. Certain
  • 1974. The undertaking was nationalised under the Sick Textile Undertakings (Nationalisation) Act, 1974. Certain
  • 39th Amendment, 1975. That Act was placed in the Ninth Schedule as Entry 105. Certain
  • 42nd Amendment, 1976. Section 4 widened Article 31C; section 55 added clauses (4) and (5) to Article 368.
  • Petition. Shareholders and creditors challenged the 1971 order, the 1974 Act, the 39th Amendment so far as it scheduled the Act, Article 31B, and sections 4 and 55 of the 42nd Amendment. Certain

05Procedural History

How it reached the Court

  1. Writ petitions under Article 32 filed directly in the Supreme Court.
  2. The Union raised a preliminary objection: the challenge to sections 4 and 55 was academic, since Articles 31A and 31B were enough to protect the Act. The majority rejected it — an order under section 18A had actually been made against the petitioners and the amendments stood in their way. Certain
  3. 9 May 1980 — operative order declaring both sections unconstitutional. Strong
  4. 31 July 1980 — reasons delivered. Bhagwati J. delivered a separate opinion (common to this case and Waman Rao). Strong

08Ratio Decidendi

Three propositions

Proposition 1 — limited amending power is a basic feature

Rule. A donee of a limited power cannot, by exercising that power, convert it into an unlimited one. Reasoning. Kesavananda held that Article 368 does not reach the basic structure. Clause (5) declares that there is no limitation at all. If valid, it would let Parliament repeal the Constitution and call it amendment. The power to destroy is not a power to amend. Result. Article 368(5) is beyond the amending power. Certain

Proposition 2 — judicial review is a basic feature

Rule. Amendments cannot be placed beyond judicial scrutiny. Reasoning. Clause (4) deprives citizens of the remedy under Article 32 and makes Article 13 a dead letter; and even without clause (5) it would leave the limit unenforceable. Without a court to say whether the limit is crossed, the limit is a rope of sand. Result. Article 368(4) void. Certain

Proposition 3 — harmony between Parts III and IV is a basic feature

Rule. The Constitution is founded on the bedrock of balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles; to give absolute primacy to one over the other disturbs that harmony. Reasoning. Part IV sets the goals; Part III sets the permissible means. The amended Article 31C withdrew Articles 14 and 19 from the whole field of legislation that could be linked to any Directive Principle — the bulk of all legislation. Articles 14, 19 and 21 form a golden triangle; removing two sides destroys it. Result. Section 4 void (4:1). Certain

09Obiter & Observations

What is not binding

  • The majority's remarks on what the original Article 31C means and its relationship to Article 39(b)–(c) were later doubted in Sanjeev Coke Mfg. Co. v. Bharat Coking Coal Ltd. (1983), which questioned whether the validity of section 4 had needed to be decided at all. Strong
  • Bhagwati J.'s criticism of the manner in which the order of 9 May 1980 was made before reasons were ready is comment, not law. Strong
  • The description of Parts III and IV as together forming the "conscience of the Constitution" (borrowed from Granville Austin) is descriptive. Strong

12Precedent Chain

Where the case sits in the line

Later developments — not part of this judgment

  • Waman Rao v. Union of India (1981). Decided by the same Bench in the same period: Articles 31A, 31B and the unamended 31C upheld; Ninth Schedule additions after 24 April 1973 open to basic-structure challenge. Strong
  • Sanjeev Coke (1983). A five-judge Bench expressed reservations about Minerva Mills on Article 31C. Strong
  • I.R. Coelho (2007). Nine Judges adopted the golden-triangle reasoning for testing Ninth Schedule laws. Strong
  • Property Owners Association (2024). Nine Judges held that striking down the 1976 widening revived the unamended text. Strong
  • The text today. Clauses (4) and (5) still appear in the printed Article 368 and the 1976 words still appear in Article 31C, with a footnote recording this judgment. They were never repealed by Parliament.

13Dissent & Concurring Opinions

Bhagwati J.'s separate opinion

  • Concurred that Article 368(4) and (5) are unconstitutional — judicial review and the limited nature of the amending power are both basic features. Certain
  • Dissented on Article 31C: the amended Article does not damage the basic structure. Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles have equal standing; neither is superior. Certain
  • Reading. The amended Article protects only a law that genuinely gives effect to a Directive Principle — one with a real and substantial connection to it; incidental provisions lose protection. Certain
  • On procedure. He recorded that the question was academic on the facts. Certain

Trap

Bhagwati J. is not a dissenter on Article 368(4)–(5). He is in a five-judge unanimity there.

14Final Holding

The operative result

  • Section 55 of the 42nd Amendment — inserting Article 368(4) and (5) — is beyond the amending power and void, since it removes all limitations on that power and so damages the basic structure. Certain
  • Section 4 of the 42nd Amendment — amending Article 31C — is beyond the amending power and void, since it damages the basic features and destroys the basic structure by totally excluding challenge under Articles 14 and 19. Certain

15Directions & Relief

What was ordered

16AOR-Specific Importance

Why an AOR candidate must own this case

  • Pleading basic structure. It supplies three named features — limited amending power, judicial review, balance of Parts III and IV — that can be pleaded directly.
  • Ouster clauses. Authority that a constitutional ouster of judicial review is itself reviewable; pairs with Kihoto Hollohan (para 7) and L. Chandra Kumar.
  • Reading down has limits. The Court will not rewrite a provision whose purpose is plain — useful when opposing a "read it down" defence.
  • Academic questions. The treatment of the preliminary objection is the Indian authority on when a constitutional question is live.
  • Directive Principles in argument. Use it for the proposition that Part IV goals must be pursued by Part III means.

172-Mark Questions

Answer in two or three lines

Q1Which provisions were struck down in Minerva Mills?

Sections 4 and 55 of the Constitution (42nd Amendment) Act, 1976 — the widened Article 31C and Article 368(4) and (5).

Q2What is the "golden triangle"?

Articles 14, 19 and 21, which together stand between the citizen and arbitrary State power.

Q3State the Bench and who wrote the majority.

Five Judges; Chandrachud C.J. for himself, Gupta, Untwalia and Kailasam JJ.; Bhagwati J. separately.

Q4What did Article 368(5) declare?

That there shall be no limitation whatever on the constituent power of Parliament to amend by addition, variation or repeal.

Q5On what point did Bhagwati J. dissent?

On Article 31C — he held the amended Article valid.

Q6Why was the preliminary objection rejected?

Because an order under section 18A had actually been passed against the petitioners; the question was not academic.

185-Mark Questions

Answer in about 150 words

Q1Explain "limited amending power is itself a basic feature".

Kesavananda held that Article 368 does not extend to altering the basic structure. Section 55 of the 42nd Amendment inserted clause (5) declaring that the amending power has no limitation whatever. The Court held that Parliament, being a donee of a limited power, cannot by exercise of that very power convert it into an absolute one. If it could, the limitation would be illusory and the Constitution could be destroyed under the guise of amendment. The limited character of the power is therefore itself part of the basic structure, and clause (5) falls. Clause (4), which barred courts from examining amendments, falls with it because a limit that no court can enforce is no limit.

Q2How did the majority describe the relationship between Parts III and IV?

As one of harmony and balance. Part IV states the ends; Part III the means. Both together form the core of the Constitution. To destroy the guarantees in Part III in order to achieve the goals of Part IV subverts the Constitution. The amended Article 31C gave Directive Principles absolute primacy over Articles 14 and 19 and so destroyed the balance, which is a basic feature.

Q3Compare the majority and Bhagwati J. on Article 31C.
  • Majority: amended 31C abrogates Articles 14 and 19 over most legislation; cannot be read down; void.
  • Bhagwati J.: Parts III and IV are equal; primacy to Part IV does not damage basic structure; 31C protects only laws with a real and substantial nexus to a Directive Principle, which courts can test; valid.
Q4What is the present status of Article 31C?

The 1976 widening is void (Minerva Mills). The original version, limited to Article 39(b) and (c) and shorn of the declaration clause (Kesavananda), remains in force, as confirmed by nine Judges in Property Owners Association (2024). Strong

19Long-Answer Questions

Structure for a 15–20 mark answer

Q1"Minerva Mills completed what Kesavananda began." Discuss.

Introduction. Case, Bench, date; the two provisions; the two votes. Background. Kesavananda; Indira Gandhi; Emergency; 42nd Amendment as Parliament's reply. Section 55. Text of Art. 368(4), (5); reasoning; limited power and judicial review as basic features; 5:0. Section 4. Original and amended 31C; majority reasoning — balance, golden triangle, no reading down; Bhagwati J.'s dissent. Preliminary objection. Why the question was live. Later history. Waman Rao; Sanjeev Coke; I.R. Coelho; Property Owners (2024). Evaluation. Did the Court need to decide 31C? Is "balance" a workable standard? Conclusion. The limit on amending power is now beyond amendment.

Q2Discuss the relationship between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles with reference to decided cases.

Phase 1 — subordination. Champakam Dorairajan (1951): Part IV runs subsidiary to Part III. Phase 2 — harmonious construction. Kerala Education Bill (1958); Kesavananda (Part IV as part of basic structure for some judges). Phase 3 — primacy by amendment. 25th Amendment (Art. 31C); 42nd Amendment widening. Phase 4 — balance. Minerva Mills: harmony is a basic feature; neither Part is supreme. Phase 5 — integration. Directive Principles read into Article 21 (Unni Krishnan, Olga Tellis). Conclusion. Ends in Part IV, means in Part III.

22Top 10 Exam Traps

Ten ways to lose marks

  1. Giving a single vote (4:1) for the whole case.
  2. Saying Article 31C was struck down entirely.
  3. Calling Bhagwati J. a dissenter on Article 368(4)–(5).
  4. Citing the 44th Amendment as having repealed clauses (4) and (5) — it did not.
  5. Saying the Court upheld or struck down the Nationalisation Act in this judgment.
  6. Attributing the golden triangle to Maneka Gandhi — the phrase is from here.
  7. Writing that Directive Principles are subordinate to Fundamental Rights — the holding is balance.
  8. Confusing section 4 with section 55.
  9. Forgetting the preliminary objection and its rejection.
  10. Stating that Minerva Mills is "no longer good law" on 31C without the 2024 nine-judge clarification.

How to hold it in memory
