AOR Paper IVLeading Case 14 of 86Master Brief

Githa Hariharan v. Reserve Bank of India

A bank refused a mother's signature for her own son. The Court did not strike the statute down — it read one word differently. "After" the father means in his absence, not after his death.

(1999) 2 SCC 228AIR 1999 SC 1149[1999] 1 SCR 66917 February 19993 Judges · unanimous, two opinions

Read this first — the section was read down, not struck down

The petitioners asked the Court to strike down section 6(a) of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 as violating Articles 14 and 15. The Court refused to strike it down and instead interpreted it so that it conforms to the Constitution. An answer that says the section was "declared unconstitutional" is wrong.

The father still comes first. The judgment does not make the mother an equal natural guardian in all circumstances. She may act as natural guardian during the father's lifetime only where he is "absent" in the wide sense the Court gave that word. This limit is the main criticism of the case and has not been removed by Parliament. Strong

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Trigger. Githa Hariharan and her husband applied to the Reserve Bank of India for Relief Bonds in their minor son's name, the mother signing as guardian. The RBI returned it: bring the father's signature or a guardianship certificate.
  2. Second petition. In a separate matrimonial dispute, a husband claimed to be the only natural guardian of the child.
  3. Law. Section 6(a), HMG Act: the natural guardian of a Hindu minor boy or unmarried girl is "the father, and after him, the mother". Section 19(b), Guardians and Wards Act is to like effect.
  4. Issue. Do these provisions discriminate on the ground of sex, contrary to Articles 14 and 15?
  5. Rule. A statute is presumed constitutional; of two possible readings, the one that keeps it valid is preferred.
  6. Ratio. "After" means "in the absence of" — not "after the lifetime of". Absence covers indifference, agreement between the parents, and physical or mental incapacity.
  7. Holding. Provisions upheld as so read. RBI wrong to refuse. Ruling prospective.

03One-Line Ratio

The sentence to reproduce

The ratio in one line

In section 6(a) of the Hindu Minority and Guardianship Act, 1956 and section 19(b) of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890, the word "after" means "in the absence of"; where the father is absent from the care of the minor for any reason — indifference, agreement, or incapacity — the mother can act as natural guardian even during his lifetime, and so read the provisions do not offend Articles 14 and 15. Certain

04Facts

What actually happened

The first petition — the bonds

  • Githa Hariharan, a writer, and her husband Dr. Mohan Ram jointly applied to the Reserve Bank of India for 9% Relief Bonds in the name of their minor son, stating that the mother would act as guardian for the investment. Strong
  • The RBI refused to accept the application and advised the couple to produce either an application signed by the father or a certificate of guardianship from a competent authority in favour of the mother. Certain
  • The parents challenged the RBI's stand and the provisions behind it under Article 32.

The second petition — the custody dispute

  • A wife, in proceedings for divorce and custody of a minor son, faced her husband's claim that he alone was the natural guardian under section 6(a) and that no decision about the child could be taken without his permission. Certain
  • She too challenged the provision.

05Procedural History

How it reached the Court

  1. Both matters came directly under Article 32; there was no judgment below.
  2. They were heard together because both turned on the meaning of section 6(a).
  3. Two opinions delivered on 17 February 1999 — the head-note records Banerjee J.'s opinion and a supplementing opinion of Anand C.J.I. for himself and Srinivasan J. Certain

08Ratio Decidendi

Four propositions

1 — Presumption of constitutionality and reading down

Rule. A statute is presumed valid; where a provision is capable of two constructions, the one that makes it constitutional is adopted. A narrow, pedantic reading that would bring the provision into conflict with the Constitution is to be avoided. Result. The Court would not strike down section 6(a) if it could reasonably be read consistently with Articles 14 and 15. Certain

2 — Both parents are natural guardians

The definitions of "guardian" and "natural guardian" do not discriminate. Both father and mother owe the duty of care; the mother's right to act does not stand suspended for the whole of the father's life. Gender equality is a basic principle of the Constitution, and the father has no preferential right merely as father. Certain

3 — "After" means "in the absence of"

The word is not confined to "after the lifetime of". "Absence" refers to the father's absence from the care of the minor's person or property for any reason whatever. The mother can act validly as natural guardian where: Certain

  • the father is wholly indifferent to the minor's affairs, even if living with the mother;
  • by mutual understanding between the parents the mother is put exclusively in charge;
  • the father is physically unable to take care of the minor — for instance by living in a different place;
  • the father is mentally incapable.

In such cases her acts are valid during the father's lifetime, and he is to be treated as "absent".

4 — Welfare of the child and international norms

The Act's object is the welfare of the minor; the word must be read in that light. Reading the provision so as to deny the mother's role would also run against CEDAW and the Beijing Declaration, to which India is a party; courts should construe domestic law in harmony with them. Certain

12Precedent Chain

Where the case sits in the line

Later developments — not part of this judgment

  • ABC v. State (NCT of Delhi) (2015) — an unwed mother may apply for sole guardianship without naming or notifying the father. Strong
  • Law Commission of India, 133rd Report (1989) and 257th Report (2015) recommended amending section 6(a) so that both parents are natural guardians equally; not enacted. Strong
  • Administrative practice. Passport, school and banking rules have since been changed to accept the mother's name and signature. Strong

14Final Holding

The operative result

  • Section 6(a) of the HMG Act and section 19(b) of the GW Act are not unconstitutional when construed as above. Certain
  • The mother can act as natural guardian of the minor during the father's lifetime where he is "absent" in the sense explained. Certain
  • The RBI was not right in insisting on the father's signature or a court order; the application ought to have been accepted on the parents' joint statement. Certain

15Directions & Relief

What was ordered

  1. The Reserve Bank of India and similar organisations to formulate appropriate methodology and forms consistent with the judgment. Certain
  2. The custody and guardianship dispute in the second petition to be decided by the District Court in the light of the judgment. Certain
  3. The judgment to operate prospectively; decisions already rendered by competent courts and past transactions not to be reopened on its basis. Certain

16AOR-Specific Importance

Why an AOR candidate must own this case

  • The model of reading down. When challenging or defending a provision under Articles 14 and 15, this is the authority for asking the Court to interpret rather than invalidate — and for resisting a strike-down prayer.
  • International conventions in argument. With Vishaka, it supports citing CEDAW to construe a statute.
  • Drafting relief. Note the form of relief — a declaration of meaning, a direction to the authority to revise its procedure, and prospective operation. Pray for these in the alternative to striking down.
  • Family practice. Applications by a mother for passports, admissions, investments or sale of a minor's property can rely on the "absence" categories; plead the facts of absence specifically.
  • Limits. Be ready for the counter-argument that the father remains the primary guardian where he is actively in charge.
  • Links. Vishaka (13), Shayara Bano (42), Joseph Shine (50), Navtej Johar (47) — the gender-equality line on the list.

172-Mark Questions

Answer in two or three lines

Q1What did the RBI refuse, and why?

An application for Relief Bonds in a minor's name signed by the mother as guardian; it required the father's signature or a guardianship certificate, treating the father as the natural guardian.

Q2How did the Court interpret "after" in section 6(a)?

As "in the absence of" the father, not "after the lifetime of" the father.

Q3Was section 6(a) struck down?

No. It was upheld by reading it down to conform to Articles 14 and 15.

Q4Give three situations in which the mother can act as natural guardian while the father is alive.

Father's indifference to the minor; mutual agreement placing the mother in charge; father's physical or mental incapacity.

Q5Which other statutory provision was construed alongside?

Section 19(b) of the Guardians and Wards Act, 1890.

Q6Does the judgment operate retrospectively?

No — prospectively.

185-Mark Questions

Answer in about 150 words

Q1Why did the Court read down rather than strike down section 6(a)?

Because of the presumption of constitutionality. A court does not invalidate legislation if a construction is reasonably available that keeps it within constitutional limits. Section 6(a) could be read in two ways: "after" as "after the lifetime of", which would postpone the mother to the father on the ground of sex alone and violate Articles 14 and 15; or "after" as "in the absence of", which treats both as natural guardians and lets the mother act whenever the father is not in charge. The second reading accords with the Act's object — the welfare of the minor — with the definition clauses, with the earlier decision in Jijabai, and with India's obligations under CEDAW. The Court therefore adopted it and upheld the section.

Q2Explain the meaning given to "absence".

Absence is not physical absence alone, nor death. It is the father's absence from the care of the minor's person or property for any reason whatever. It includes cases where the father, though living with the family, is wholly indifferent; where by agreement the mother is exclusively in charge; and where he cannot look after the minor because he lives elsewhere, or is physically or mentally incapable. In each the mother's acts as guardian are valid.

Q3What role did international conventions play?

The Court noted that India has ratified CEDAW and subscribed to the Beijing Declaration, which require equality of men and women in matters relating to their children. Domestic courts are under an obligation to give due regard to such instruments when construing domestic law where there is no inconsistency. The interpretation adopted gives effect to those principles.

Q4What is the main criticism of the decision?

That it stops short of equality. The father remains the natural guardian by default; the mother's status is contingent on showing his "absence". Law Commission reports have recommended amending the section to make both parents equal natural guardians. Strong

22Top 10 Exam Traps

Ten ways to lose marks

  2. Saying the mother is now an equal natural guardian in every case.
  3. Limiting "absence" to physical absence.
  4. Forgetting section 19(b) of the GW Act.
  5. Omitting the second petition.
  6. Saying the judgment is retrospective.
  7. Calling it a Constitution Bench.
  8. Missing CEDAW.
  9. Attributing the case to "State Bank" rather than the Reserve Bank.
