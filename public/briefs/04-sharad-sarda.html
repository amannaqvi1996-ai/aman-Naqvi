AOR Paper IVLeading CaseMaster Brief №2

Sharad Birdhichand Sardav. State of Maharashtra

The Panchsheel of circumstantial evidence — and the case in which a confirmed death sentence was set aside because one link in the chain would not close.

(1984) 4 SCC 116AIR 1984 SC 1622[1985] 1 SCR 8817 July 19843 Judges · 2:1

§The 60-Second Recall

If you read nothing else

  1. Facts. Manju married the appellant on 11 February 1982. Four months later, on the night of 11/12 June 1982, she died in Pune of potassium cyanide. Prosecution: the husband poisoned her. Defence: she took her own life. No eyewitness — a wholly circumstantial case.
  2. The Panchsheel. Restating Hanumant (1952), the Court laid down five conditions every circumstantial case must satisfy, and gave them their name: the "panchsheel of the proof of a case based on circumstantial evidence."
  3. "May be" is not "must be." Circumstances must be fully established — "must be or should be", never "may be". Suspicion, however great, cannot take the place of legal proof.
  4. Section 32(1) widened. Manju's letters to her sister were admitted. Indian law goes far beyond English law: statements about the circumstances of the transaction count, the declarant need not have expected to die, and the section covers suicide as well as homicide.
  5. Result. Appeal allowed 2:1 — Fazal Ali and Sabyasachi Mukharji JJ. acquitting, Varadarajan J. dissenting. The death sentence, confirmed by the High Court, was set aside. Possession of the poison was never proved and the hypothesis of suicide was never excluded.
  6. For your sitting. Section 32 of the Evidence Act is now Section 26, Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam 2023. Cite both. See §12.

02Facts & Timeline

Four months from wedding to post-mortem

Manju married the appellant and moved into a flat in Pune. Over the four months that followed she wrote letters to her sister and a friend describing her treatment in her husband's house — by her own account she was kept there, as the Court put it, like an unpaid servant. The prosecution case was that the appellant, who was alleged to be involved with another woman, administered potassium cyanide on the night of 11/12 June. The defence was that Manju, unhappy and without hope, took it herself. Those same letters were the centre of both cases.

03Court Ladder

Three courts, three different answers — watch the attrition

Trap

The High Court did not merely uphold a conviction — it confirmed a death sentence. Saying so shows you know the stakes and the posture. And note the appellant reached the Supreme Court having lost twice; the acquittal came from the top only.

04Issues

Frame in this order

  1. What must the prosecution establish before a conviction can rest on circumstantial evidence alone?
  2. Are the deceased's letters admissible under s. 32(1) — and does that clause reach statements made long before death, and deaths by suicide?
  3. In a case of murder by poisoning, what specific facts must the prosecution prove?
  4. Can a false defence be used as an additional link to complete an incomplete prosecution chain?
  5. What is the effect of circumstances never put to the accused under s. 313 CrPC?
  6. Where two views are reasonably possible, which prevails?

05The Panchsheel

Paras 153–154 — the most-cited passage in Indian criminal law

The five conditions were first laid down in Hanumant v. State of M.P., AIR 1952 SC 343. Sarda's contribution was to restate them with a crucial gloss on the first, to insist they are cumulative, and to give them the name that stuck.

The Court's own name for it

"These five golden principles, if we may say so, constitute the panchsheel of the proof of a case based on circumstantial evidence."

06May Be / Must Be

The gloss on the first principle — worth a mark on its own

The first principle says circumstances must be fully established. Sarda stresses that the word is "must be" or "should be" — not "may be" — and that the difference is legal, not merely grammatical, adopting the celebrated passage from Shivaji Sahabrao Bobade v. State of Maharashtra, (1973) 2 SCC 793.

07Section 32(1)

Where Indian law leaves English law behind — now s. 26 BSA

Section 32(1) makes relevant a dead person's statement as to the cause of his death, or as to any of the circumstances of the transaction which resulted in his death, in cases in which the cause of that person's death comes into question. That second limb has no counterpart in English law, and Sarda is the leading authority on how far it reaches.

Trap

Do not write that a statement under s. 32(1) is a "dying declaration" in the English sense. Under Indian law the declarant need not have been dying, or expecting to die, when the statement was made. Manju's letters were written weeks before her death, about her marriage — and they came in.

08Four More Holdings

Each of these is separately examinable

1 · Murder by poisoning — what the prosecution must prove

Four things: (i) a clear motive to administer the poison; (ii) that the deceased died of that poison; (iii) that the accused had the poison in his possession; and (iv) that he had the opportunity to administer it. Remember it as M–D–P–O.

2 · A false defence cannot supply a lacuna

A false explanation by the accused may sometimes be used as an additional link — but only once the prosecution's own links are satisfactorily proved, point to guilt with reasonable definiteness, and are proximate in time and situation. It is not the law that a gap in the prosecution case can be cured by a false defence. The prosecution must stand on its own legs.

3 · Section 313 CrPC — circumstances not put to the accused go out

Any incriminating circumstance not put to the accused for explanation must be excluded from consideration altogether. In Sarda a long list of circumstances fell away on this ground alone — which is a useful reminder that this is a substantive protection, not a formality. (Now s. 351 BNSS.)

4 · Two views, one outcome

Where the evidence reasonably admits of two views — one consistent with guilt, one with innocence — the view favourable to the accused must be adopted. Here the reasonable possibility that Manju took the poison herself could not be safely excluded, and that alone was decisive.

12New Codes

The single most important update for a 2027 sitting

The three criminal codes were replaced with effect from 1 July 2024. Sarda is untouched as law, but every provision it turns on has been renumbered. In an answer, cite the new section and note the old one in brackets — it reads as current and shows the examiner you have not been revising from a stale book.

13Still Live Law

The pairing that impresses

Sarda for the standard, Neelu Koshti for its limit. A candidate who can say "the chain must be complete, but the absence of proved motive does not by itself break it" is answering at a different level from one who only recites the five.

14Twelve Traps

Read this last thing before you go in

Do not writeWrite instead
"The Supreme Court laid down the five principles for the first time."They come from Hanumant (1952). Sarda restated, sharpened and named them.
"The judgment was unanimous."2:1. Varadarajan J. dissented and would have upheld the conviction.
"The conviction was upheld."Acquitted. A confirmed death sentence was set aside.
"The letters were held inadmissible."They were admitted under s. 32(1) — that is the case's other great holding.
"A dying declaration requires expectation of death."Not in India. That is the English rule, expressly departed from.
"Section 32 applies only to homicide."It covers suicide as well.
"A statement months before death is too remote."Distance of time alone is not fatal; ask whether it is part of the same continuous transaction.
"A false defence completes the chain."Only as an additional link once the prosecution's own links are proved. It can never fill a gap.
"Motive was the missing link."Possession of the poison was. And the suicide hypothesis was never excluded.
"It was a Constitution Bench."Three Judges.
"Section 32 of the Evidence Act."In 2027, s. 26 BSA 2023 — with the old section noted in brackets.
"Panchsheel is a textbook nickname."It is the Court's own word, in the judgment.

16Memory Hooks

Built for recall under time pressure

The case in seven words

Cyanide · No possession · No exclusion · Acquitted.

The five principles

FULLY · ONLY · CONCLUSIVE · EXCLUDE · CHAIN. Fully established → consistent only with guilt → conclusive in nature → exclude every other hypothesis → a complete chain. Note that the last three are one idea tested three ways; if you blank, reason from the chain backwards.

The poisoning requirements

M–D–P–O — Motive, Death by that poison, Possession, Opportunity. In Sarda the P failed.

The bench

Two let him go, one would not. Fazal Ali and Mukharji acquit; Varadarajan dissents.

Section 32(1) against England

India is wider on three counts: subject-matter (circumstances of the transaction), state of mind (no expectation of death), and manner of death (suicide too).

The renumbering

32 → 26. "Thirty-two becomes twenty-six." The one number to get right in 2027.

17Question Drill

Six framings and the skeleton each one wants

1. "State and explain the five golden principles of circumstantial evidence."

Skeleton: Source in Hanumant → restatement in Sarda at paras 153–154 (quote them) → the "must be / may be" gloss with Bobade → they are cumulative, the chain metaphor → apply to Sarda's own facts to show the fourth principle doing the work → note the modern limit in Neelu Koshti on motive.

2. "Suspicion, however great, cannot take the place of legal proof.' Discuss."

Skeleton: The proposition → why circumstantial cases are where it bites → the certainty scale from conjecture to "must be" → benefit of doubt and the two-views rule → Sarda as the illustration: a confirmed death sentence set aside because possession was unproved.

3. "Explain the scope of s. 32(1) IEA / s. 26 BSA. How does Indian law differ from English law?"

Skeleton: Text of the clause, both limbs → the three points of departure from England → proximity as continuity of transaction, not a fixed period → suicide included → Sarda's letters as the illustration → conclude on why the legislature widened it.

4. Problem: a young wife dies in the matrimonial home; the case is circumstantial; she left letters.

Skeleton: Admit the letters under s. 26 BSA (state the gates) → list the circumstances the prosecution relies on → test each against principle 1 (fully established?) → then principle 4 (is any innocent hypothesis left standing?) → check s. 351 BNSS compliance → if two views, acquit. Cite Sarda and Chetan Dashrath Gade.

5. "Can a false defence complete the prosecution's chain?"

Skeleton: No as a general rule → the three preconditions before it can even be an additional link → the prosecution must stand on its own legs → Sarda held the High Court erred precisely here.

6. "State the ratio decidendi."

One sentence: A conviction resting wholly on circumstantial evidence is sustainable only where every circumstance is fully established, points only to guilt, is conclusive, excludes every other reasonable hypothesis, and forms a chain so complete that innocence is not a reasonable conclusion — and where any one of these fails, the accused is entitled to acquittal.
