Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan

The judgment that built binding law out of an international convention because Parliament had written none — and then said so, expressly, under Article 141.

(1997) 6 SCC 241AIR 1997 SC 3011[1997] 3 Suppl. SCR 40413 August 19973 Judges · unanimous

Read this first — it changes how you answer

The Vishaka Guidelines are no longer the operative law. The Court itself said they would bind only "until suitable legislation is enacted to occupy the field." That legislation came: the Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013, in force from 9 December 2013. In a 2027 answer, Vishaka is the constitutional authority and the source of the definition; the POSH Act is the governing regime. §12 and §15 keep the two apart, and a comparison table shows exactly what changed.

On sources. No judgment file was uploaded, so priority 1 in your hierarchy was unavailable. I worked from the judgment text as published, the Supreme Court's AOR leading-case material, and authoritative reports. Every proposition carries a confidence tag; §30 lists what I could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Trigger. A brutal gang rape of a social worker in a Rajasthan village. The Court expressly declined to go into that incident — it was the subject of separate criminal proceedings — and treated it only as the occasion for the constitutional question.
  2. Petition. A class action under Article 32 by social activists and NGOs, seeking enforcement of the fundamental rights of working women.
  3. The gap. Sexual harassment at the workplace violates Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 — but there was no enacted law on the subject.
  4. The move. Where there is a void in domestic law, international conventions — here CEDAW — that are not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and are in harmony with their spirit must be read into them to enlarge their meaning and content. Constitutional hooks: Article 51(c) and Article 253 with Entry 14 of the Union List.
  5. The output. Twelve guidelines and norms — including the first Indian definition of sexual harassment, the employer's duty, and a Complaints Committee headed by a woman with not less than half women members and a third-party NGO member.
  6. The status. Declared to be law under Article 141, binding and enforceable — until suitable legislation is enacted to occupy the field.
  7. Held. Guidelines issued; unanimous; no dissent. The Solicitor General supported them.
  8. Significance. The clearest Indian authority on filling a legislative vacuum by judicial declaration, and on using unincorporated international law as an aid to constitutional construction. Superseded in operation by the POSH Act 2013.

03One-Line Ratio

Precise, and not simplified past the point of accuracy

One-line ratio

Sexual harassment of a woman at her workplace violates her fundamental rights under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21; and where there is a void in domestic law, international conventions and norms which are not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and are in harmony with their spirit must be read into those Articles to enlarge their meaning and content — so that the Court may, under Article 32, lay down guidelines which are law under Article 141, binding and enforceable until suitable legislation occupies the field.

Exam memory hook

"No law? Then the treaty speaks through Article 21 — until Parliament speaks."

04Facts

Material facts separated from background — and note what the Court refused to decide

Background facts

  • The petition arose out of an alleged brutal gang rape of a social worker in a village in Rajasthan. 🟢 CERTAIN
  • The petitioners were social activists and non-governmental organisations, of whom Vishaka was the first. 🟢

Material facts — what the case actually rested on

  1. There was no enacted law in India governing sexual harassment of women at the workplace. 🟢 CERTAIN — the premise of the whole judgment
  2. India had ratified CEDAW and had made official commitments at the Fourth World Conference on Women at Beijing. 🟢
  3. The petition was brought not to redress the individual incident but as a class action, to secure the fundamental rights of working women generally. 🟢
  4. The Solicitor General appeared and supported the framing of guidelines — so there was no adversarial contest on the relief. 🟢

The most important thing the Court did NOT do

It did not adjudicate the incident. The judgment records that the incident was the subject matter of a separate criminal action and that no further mention of it was necessary. The judgment does not name the victim. If you name her in an answer, you are supplying a fact from outside the judgment — exactly the kind of import that costs marks in a leading-cases paper. 🟢 CERTAIN

05Procedural History

Short, because the case began and ended in the Supreme Court

  1. Original proceeding. A writ petition filed directly in the Supreme Court. There were no lower-court proceedings in this matter — nothing was appealed. 🟢
  2. Jurisdiction invoked. Article 32 — original jurisdiction for the enforcement of fundamental rights. Not Article 136, not Article 226 in appeal. 🟢
  3. Character of the petition. A class action brought by activists and NGOs. Locus was not in issue: the petition sought to enforce the rights of working women as a class, which is the settled PIL position. 🟢
  4. Respondents. The State of Rajasthan and the Union of India. 🟢
  5. Posture at hearing. Not adversarial on the relief — the Solicitor General supported the guidelines and assisted the Court in framing them. 🟢
  6. Procedural question before the Court. Whether, in a petition under Article 32 and in the absence of legislation, the Court could frame binding norms — and if so, from what source that bindingness came. The answer was Article 141. 🟢

Contrast worth carrying

Antulay reached the Court after four courts and six years; Vishaka had no procedural history at all. That difference is itself examinable: Article 32 is an original jurisdiction, and a petitioner need not exhaust anything before invoking it for the enforcement of a fundamental right.

06Issues

Kept separate by category

Constitutional

Issue 1. Does sexual harassment of a woman at her workplace violate her fundamental rights?
Provisions: Arts. 14, 15, 19(1)(g), 21. Answer: Yes — all four. Equality, non-discrimination on the ground of sex, the right to practise any profession or carry on any occupation, and the right to life with dignity. 🟢

Issue 2. Is a safe working environment part of the right under Article 19(1)(g)?
Answer: Yes — the right to carry on an occupation depends on a workplace free of harassment; harassment is a disabling condition on the exercise of the right. 🟢

International law in domestic courts

Issue 3. May the Court use an international convention that has not been enacted into municipal law to construe the fundamental rights?
Provisions: Arts. 51(c), 253, Entry 14 List I. Answer: Yes, where there is a void in domestic law and the convention is not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and is in harmony with their spirit. 🟢

Issue 4. Does this make the convention itself directly enforceable?
Answer: No. It is an aid to construction that enlarges the content of the existing rights — the enforceable right remains the Article, not the treaty. 🟡 STRONG — implicit in the reasoning rather than stated as a separate holding

Remedial / procedural

Issue 5. Can the Court frame guidelines in the absence of legislation, and what is their legal status?
Provisions: Arts. 32 and 141. Answer: Yes; they are law declared by the Supreme Court under Article 141, binding and enforceable. 🟢

Issue 6. For how long do they operate?
Answer: Until suitable legislation is enacted to occupy the field. 🟢 — and this is the clause that matters most today

Substantive content

Issue 7. What is sexual harassment, and what duties does it place on an employer?
Answer: Defined in the guidelines; the employer bears a positive duty of prevention, redressal and prosecution assistance. 🟢

08Ratio Decidendi

Four propositions, each with rule, reasoning, application and conclusion

Proposition 1 — Sexual harassment at work is a violation of fundamental rights

Rule. It infringes Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21. Reasoning. It denies equality of terms; it is discrimination based on sex; it disables the exercise of the right to carry on an occupation; and it is an assault on dignity, which Article 21 protects. Application. Working women had no protection against it. Conclusion. The State was under a constitutional obligation to secure them against it. 🟢 CERTAIN

Proposition 2 — International norms may be read into the fundamental rights where domestic law is silent

Rule. International conventions and norms are significant for construing the guarantees of gender equality where there is a void in domestic law, provided they are not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and are in harmony with their spirit. Reasoning. Article 51(c) directs the State to foster respect for international obligations; Article 253 with Entry 14, List I shows the Constitution contemplates their domestic implementation. Application. CEDAW, ratified by India, supplied the content. Conclusion. The Articles were read to include a guarantee against harassment at work. 🟢 CERTAIN

Proposition 3 — The Court may frame binding guidelines to fill a legislative vacuum

Rule. In exercise of the power under Article 32, the Court may lay down guidelines and norms, and these are law declared under Article 141. Reasoning. Article 32 guarantees not merely a forum but an effective remedy; a right without machinery is illusory. Application. Twelve heads were framed and the Solicitor General supported them. Conclusion. They were binding and enforceable. 🟢 CERTAIN

Proposition 4 — Such guidelines are provisional by design

Rule. They operate only until suitable legislation is enacted to occupy the field. Reasoning. The Court was supplying a want, not displacing the legislature. Application. The POSH Act 2013 occupied the field. Conclusion. The guidelines' operative life ended; their interpretive authority did not. 🟢 CERTAIN as to the rule; the 2013 consequence is a post-judgment development

Do not overclaim

Vishaka does not hold that unincorporated treaties are directly enforceable in India. It holds that where domestic law is silent, they inform the construction of existing constitutional guarantees. The enforceable right is the Article; the convention supplies its content. Blurring that line is the single most common error in essays on this case.

10Judicial Reasoning

Provision → precedent → interpretation → application → conclusion

The competing positions

Argument A. India follows the dualist tradition: a treaty binds the State internationally but has no domestic effect until Parliament enacts it under Article 253. For a court to give CEDAW domestic force is to legislate.

Argument B. The Court is not enforcing the treaty. Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 already exist and already prohibit what is complained of; the convention merely shows what those Articles mean in a context Parliament has not addressed. Article 51(c) makes that use of international material constitutionally proper, and Article 32 obliges the Court to make the right effective.

Resolution. Argument B, with the void as its precondition and consistency with Part III as its ceiling — and with an express expiry: the guidelines yield the moment Parliament legislates. That last feature is what makes the judgment defensible against the charge of judicial legislation, and it is the point to make if asked to evaluate it.

13Dissent & Concurring Opinions

There are none — and that is itself the answer

Not established from the judgment — because it does not exist

The judgment was delivered by Verma, C.J.I., for the Court. There is no dissent and no separate or concurring opinion. Sujata V. Manohar and B.N. Kirpal, JJ. joined the single judgment. 🟢 CERTAIN

If a question asks you to discuss the dissent, the correct answer is that there was none — and the more interesting observation is why: the Solicitor General supported the guidelines, so the Court was not resolving a contest but supplying a want with the Union's assistance. That absence of adversarial opposition is part of what makes the judgment unusual, and it is the honest thing to say if asked to evaluate its legitimacy.

Contrast to carry across cases

Antulay (7 Judges, divided, contested) and Vishaka (3 Judges, unanimous, uncontested) sit at opposite ends of how the Supreme Court makes law. An examiner asking about judicial law-making will reward a candidate who can put those two side by side.

14Final Holding

In precise legal language

Held

  1. Sexual harassment of a woman at her workplace violates her fundamental rights under Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21, and each incident results in a violation of those rights.
  2. Gender equality includes protection from sexual harassment and the right to work with dignity, which is a universally recognised human right.
  3. In the absence of domestic law occupying the field, the contents of international conventions and norms are significant for the purpose of construing the constitutional guarantees, provided they are not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and are in harmony with their spirit.
  4. It is the duty of the employer or other responsible person in a workplace or institution to prevent or deter sexual harassment and to provide procedures for resolution, settlement or prosecution.
  5. Accordingly, guidelines and norms are laid down for observance at all workplaces until suitable legislation is enacted.
  6. These guidelines are declared to be law under Article 141 and are binding and enforceable in law.

Result

Writ petition allowed in the terms of the guidelines. No compensation, no adjudication of the underlying incident, and no direction against any individual respondent. The relief was normative and prospective. 🟢

How to phrase it in an answer

"Vishaka laid down guidelines which, by their own terms, were to hold the field only until Parliament legislated. 16AOR-Specific Importance

Advocate-on-Record mode

  • Article 32 as an original jurisdiction. No exhaustion of remedies, no appeal, no lower-court record. Know that a Part III right can be brought straight to the Supreme Court, and that this is not a discretionary leave jurisdiction like Article 136.
  • Locus in a class action. The petitioners had suffered nothing personally. Relaxed locus for the enforcement of the rights of a class is settled, and Vishaka is a clean example to cite in a maintainability argument.
  • Article 141 as a source of enforceable norms. This is the case that shows guidelines can be law. When you draft a prayer seeking directions in the nature of guidelines, this is your authority — and you should plead the vacuum, because the vacuum is the precondition.
  • Moulding of relief under Article 32. The Court gave no compensation and no individual remedy. Relief in a PIL is normative and prospective; frame the prayer accordingly rather than asking for damages.
  • The limits of judicial law-making. The guidelines carried their own expiry. If you are resisting a prayer for guidelines, this is the line: courts supply a want, they do not occupy a field Parliament has taken.
  • International conventions in pleadings. To rely on an unincorporated treaty you must plead (i) the void, (ii) consistency with Part III, and (iii) harmony with its spirit — the three gates in §10. Pleading the treaty alone will not do.
  • Drafting leverage. A writ petition modelled on Vishaka pleads: the fundamental rights engaged; the absence of any statutory or other machinery; India's international obligations and the constitutional provisions admitting them; the class affected; and a prayer for directions to operate until legislation.
  • Cross-paper value. Paper IV as the leading case; Paper II because an Article 32 class-action petition is a standard drafting question and this case supplies the structure; Paper I on Article 32 and Article 141.

185-Mark Questions

Model answer, essential points, and the common mistake

Q1How may an international convention be used to construe fundamental rights?

Model answer. Where there is a void in domestic law, the contents of international conventions and norms are significant for construing the constitutional guarantees, provided they are not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and are in harmony with their spirit. The warrant is Article 51(c), which directs the State to foster respect for international law and treaty obligations, read with Article 253 and Entry 14 of List I, which show that the Constitution contemplates their domestic implementation. In Vishaka, CEDAW was used to enlarge the meaning and content of Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21. The convention does not thereby become directly enforceable; it is an aid to construction.

Essential points: the void; the two conditions; the two constitutional hooks; the limit.

Common mistake: writing that Vishaka made CEDAW enforceable in India. It did not.

Q2What is the legal status of the Vishaka Guidelines today?

Model answer. They were declared to be law under Article 141, binding and enforceable, but expressly only until suitable legislation was enacted to occupy the field. The POSH Act 2013 occupied it with effect from 9 December 2013. The guidelines therefore no longer operate as the governing regime. They remain the constitutional foundation of the law, and the statutory definition in section 2(n) substantially reproduces the definition the Court framed, so Vishaka continues to matter for interpretation.

Essential points: Art. 141; the expiry clause; the 2013 Act; what survives.

Common mistake: answering as though the guidelines are still the operative law.

Q3Why is Article 19(1)(g) engaged?

Model answer. Article 19(1)(g) guarantees the right to practise any profession or to carry on any occupation, trade or business. Sexual harassment at the workplace makes the exercise of that right conditional on enduring it, or forces the woman to abandon the occupation altogether. A safe working environment is therefore not incidental to the right but part of its content. This is the limb that distinguishes Vishaka from a case argued on dignity alone.

Essential points: the text of 19(1)(g); harassment as a disabling condition; safe environment as content of the right.

Common mistake: arguing only Articles 21 and 14 and dropping 19(1)(g) — which is the most distinctive part of the holding.

Q4Was Vishaka an exercise in judicial legislation?

Model answer. The charge has force: the Court framed a detailed code of twelve heads, including institutional machinery, in a field Parliament had not entered. Three answers are available. First, the rights already existed; the Court supplied machinery, not the right. Second, Article 32 guarantees an effective remedy, and a right without machinery is illusory. Third, and most important, the Court built in its own expiry — the guidelines were to yield the moment Parliament legislated, which they did in 2013. That self-limitation distinguishes supplying a want from usurping a function. The criticism is nonetheless worth stating, and a balanced answer states both.

Essential points: the charge; the three answers; the expiry clause as the strongest.

Common mistake: defending the judgment without acknowledging the objection. Examiners want the tension, not a brief for one side.

Q5What duties does the employer bear under the guidelines?

Model answer. An express duty to prevent or deter sexual harassment and to provide procedures for resolution, settlement or prosecution. Concretely: express prohibition notified and publicised; inclusion in service and conduct rules; work conditions that do not create a hostile environment; a time-bound complaint mechanism independent of any service-rule violation; a Complaints Committee headed by a woman with not less than half women members and a third-party NGO member; assistance in initiating criminal proceedings; disciplinary action where the conduct is misconduct; awareness measures; and responsibility for assisting where the harassment is by a third party.

Essential points: prevention, redressal, prosecution assistance, machinery, awareness.

Common mistake: listing only the Committee and omitting the preventive and awareness duties.

19Long-Answer Questions

What the examiner is testing, and how to structure it

L1"Vishaka is the high-water mark of judicial creativity in India." Discuss.

Testing: whether you can evaluate rather than describe.

Structure: what the Court did → the constitutional basis (Arts. 32, 141, 51(c), 253) → the case for it (rights existed, remedy did not; Art. 32 demands effectiveness) → the case against (detailed institutional machinery is a legislative product; no adversarial contest; three Judges) → the self-limiting expiry clause → what actually happened in 2013 → conclusion.

Must appear: the three gates; the Art. 141 declaration; the "until legislation" clause; the POSH Act.

Extra marks: noting that the Solicitor General supported the guidelines, so the legitimacy objection is weaker than it first appears; and comparing with Antulay as the contested end of judicial law-making.

Trap: treating the guidelines as still operative.

L2Examine the place of international law in Indian constitutional interpretation.

Testing: the dualist framework and its judicial softening.

Structure: the dualist starting point (a treaty binds the State, not the courts, until enacted) → Art. 253 and Entry 14 → Art. 51(c) as a directive → Nilabati Behera → Vishaka's three gates → the limit (aid to construction, not direct enforceability) → Teoh as comparative material → conclusion on where the line now sits.

Must appear: the void requirement; consistency with Part III; harmony with its spirit.

Extra marks: pointing out that the Court relied on India's own official commitments at Beijing, which converts the argument from imposition to holding the State to its word.

L3Trace the law on sexual harassment at the workplace from 1997 to the present.

Testing: whether you keep the judgment and the statute apart.

Structure: the pre-1997 vacuum → Vishaka and the twelve guidelines → Apparel Export Promotion Council (1999) → Medha Kotwal Lele (2013) → the POSH Act 2013 and its machinery (IC, LC, timelines, penalties) → Aureliano Fernandes (2023) on implementation and on fairness to the respondent → the criminal track now under BNS s. 75 → conclusion.

Must appear: the date the statute came into force; the continuity of the definition; the IC/LC distinction.

Extra marks: the observation that the Act carries forward the Court's own definition almost verbatim.

L4What relief can the Supreme Court grant under Article 32 beyond the traditional writs?

Testing: the moulding of relief.

Structure: the five writs → compensation as a public-law remedy (Nilabati Behera) → normative guidelines binding under Art. 141 (Vishaka) → continuing mandamus and monitoring (Medha Kotwal Lele) → limits: relief must serve a Part III right and yield to legislation → conclusion.

Must appear: that Vishaka granted no individual relief at all.

Trap: assuming compensation was awarded. It was not.

25Memory Architecture

Built for recall under time pressure

Five things I must remember

  1. Three Judges, unanimous, 13 August 1997, Article 32.
  2. Four rights: 14 · 15 · 19(1)(g) · 21. The odd one out — 19(1)(g) — is the one that makes it a workplace case.
  3. Void → CEDAW → Articles 51(c) and 253 → read in. Three gates: void, not inconsistent, in harmony.
  4. Twelve guidelines, law under Article 141.
  5. "Until suitable legislation occupies the field" — and in 2013 it did.

Three things I must not forget

  1. The Court named no one and gave no individual relief.
  2. Article 141, not 142.
  3. The treaty was not made enforceable — it was used to construe.

One story that links it all

A social worker is attacked in a Rajasthan village. Activists go straight to the Supreme Court under Article 32 — not about her, but about every working woman. Three Judges find that harassment at work breaks four fundamental rights at once, and then hit a wall: Parliament has written nothing. So they look outward. India has signed CEDAW and stood up at Beijing and said these things; Article 51(c) tells the State to honour that, and Article 253 shows treaties are meant to reach home. So the convention is read into Articles 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21 — not enforced, read in. Out of it come twelve guidelines, stamped as law by Article 141 — with an expiry clause the Court writes itself: until Parliament legislates. Sixteen years later Parliament does, and the POSH Act takes over — carrying the Court's own definition with it.

Facts → Issue → Provision → Precedent → Ratio → Result, in one paragraph.

26Flashcards

Twenty cards — retrieval, not trivia

F1The three gates for reading in a convention?

A void in domestic law · not inconsistent with the fundamental rights · in harmony with their spirit.

F2The two constitutional hooks?

Article 51(c) and Article 253, with Entry 14 of List I.

F3What does reading in achieve?

It enlarges the meaning and content of existing Articles. It does not create a new right or make the treaty enforceable.

F4Which Article makes guidelines binding?

Article 141 — law declared by the Supreme Court.

F5Under which Article did the Court act?

Article 32 — original jurisdiction, class action.

F6The five limbs of the definition?

Physical contact and advances · a demand or request for sexual favours · sexually coloured remarks · showing pornography · any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature.

F7The test of "unwelcome"?

Whether the victim has a reasonable apprehension that the conduct is humiliating or constitutes a health and safety problem, or creates a hostile working environment.

F8Guideline 7 — four features?

Headed by a woman · not less than half the members women · a third-party NGO member · annual report to the Government.

F9Why a third-party member?

To prevent undue pressure or influence from senior levels — the structural safeguard against an in-house cover-up.

F10What did the Court say about the incident?

That it was the subject of a separate criminal action and no further mention of it was necessary. The victim is not named.

F11What relief did any individual get?

None. The relief was normative and prospective.

F12Was there a dissent?

No. Single unanimous judgment by Verma C.J.I.

F13Which Indian precedent was relied on?

Nilabati Behera v. State of Orissa (1993) — public-law remedy under Article 32 and the use of international instruments.

F14What role did Teoh play?

Comparative persuasive support on legitimate expectation from a ratified convention. Not the basis of the Indian holding.

F15The expiry clause, verbatim in substance?

Binding and enforceable "until suitable legislation is enacted to occupy the field."

F16What occupied the field, and when?

The POSH Act 2013 — Act 14 of 2013 — in force 9 December 2013.

F17IC versus LC?

Internal Committee inside a workplace with 10 or more employees; Local Committee constituted by the District Officer for smaller workplaces, the unorganised sector, or complaints against the employer.

F18POSH timelines?

Complaint within 3 months, extendable by 3 · inquiry within 90 days · report within 10 days · employer acts within 60 days.

F19What survives of Vishaka in the statute?

The definition — section 2(n) substantially reproduces the five limbs the Court framed — and the constitutional foundation of the whole regime.

F20The criminal offence today?

Section 75, Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023, replacing IPC s. 354A from 1 July 2024. It runs parallel to and independently of the POSH inquiry.

27Last-Night Revision Card

The section to read walking in — nothing else

Vishaka v. State of Rajasthan, (1997) 6 SCC 241 — 13 August 1997 — 3 Judges, unanimous

Facts. A class action under Article 32 by activists and NGOs, occasioned by the gang rape of a social worker in Rajasthan. The Court expressly declined to go into that incident and did not name the victim. There was no law in India on sexual harassment at the workplace.

Issues. Does workplace sexual harassment violate fundamental rights? May an unincorporated convention be used to construe them? Can the Court frame binding guidelines in a legislative vacuum, and for how long?

Provisions. Arts. 14, 15, 19(1)(g), 21 · Art. 32 (jurisdiction) · Art. 141 (bindingness) · Arts. 51(c) and 253 with Entry 14, List I (international law) · CEDAW Arts. 11 and 24.

Ratio. Sexual harassment at work violates Arts. 14, 15, 19(1)(g) and 21. Where there is a void in domestic law, international conventions not inconsistent with the fundamental rights and in harmony with their spirit must be read into them to enlarge their content. Guidelines framed under Art. 32 are law under Art. 141, binding until suitable legislation occupies the field.

Key precedent. Nilabati Behera (1993) — public-law remedy under Art. 32 and the use of international instruments. Teoh (Australia) — comparative only.

Procedural point. Original jurisdiction — no appeal, no exhaustion, relaxed locus for a class. Relief was normative and prospective; no compensation, no individual direction.

Holding. Twelve guidelines, grouped as define · prevent · redress · support · save. Guideline 7: Complaints Committee headed by a woman, not less than half the members women, a third-party NGO member, annual report to the Government.

Exception / limit. The convention was not made enforceable — it was an aid to construction. And the guidelines carried their own expiry: the POSH Act 2013, in force 9 December 2013, occupied the field. Cite Vishaka for the constitutional foundation and the definition; cite the Act for the machinery.

Trap. Not a Constitution Bench (three Judges). Article 141, not 142. No dissent. No compensation. Do not name the victim. Do not say the guidelines still govern.

One-line takeaway. No law? Then the treaty speaks through Article 21 — until Parliament speaks.
