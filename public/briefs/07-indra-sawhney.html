AOR Paper IVLeading Case 7 of 86Master Brief

Indra Sawhney v. Union of India

The Mandal judgment. Nine judges upheld 27% reservation for backward classes, capped all reservation at 50%, excluded the creamy layer, and shut the door on quotas in promotion — a door Parliament then reopened by amendment.

1992 Supp (3) SCC 217AIR 1993 SC 477[1992] Supp. 2 SCR 45416 November 19929 Judges · 6:3

Read this first — three holdings have been altered since 1992

  • Promotions. The Court said no reservation in promotion. Parliament answered with Article 16(4A) (77th Amendment, 1995), upheld in M. Nagaraj (2006) and refined in Jarnail Singh (2018) — case 49 on your list.
  • Carry-forward within 50%. Answered by Article 16(4B) (81st Amendment, 2000).
  • Economic criterion alone. The Court struck down the 10% quota. The 103rd Amendment, 2019 inserted Articles 15(6) and 16(6); upheld 3:2 in Janhit Abhiyan (2022). Strong

In an answer, state what Indra Sawhney held, then what changed. §12 keeps the two apart.

On sources. No judgment file was uploaded. I worked from the Supreme Court's head-notes volume and the settled reported position. §30 lists what I could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Trigger. On 13 August 1990 the Union issued an Office Memorandum reserving 27% of central civil posts for Socially and Educationally Backward Classes, acting on the Mandal Commission report.
  2. Second OM. On 25 September 1991 a new Government added preference for the poorer among them and a further 10% for other economically backward sections.
  3. Issue. What does Article 16(4) permit — who is a "backward class", how much may be reserved, and where?
  4. Rule. Article 16(4) is not an exception to Article 16(1) but a facet of it. Backward classes may be identified with caste as a starting point; social backwardness is the test.
  5. Limits. Creamy layer excluded. 50% ceiling, save extraordinary situations. No reservation in promotions. Economic criterion alone will not do.
  6. Holding. 27% OM upheld, subject to creamy-layer exclusion. 10% economic quota struck down.
  7. Significance. The framework within which every reservation question is still argued.

03One-Line Ratio

The sentence to reproduce

The ratio in one line

Article 16(4) is a facet of equality under Article 16(1), not an exception to it; a backward class may be identified by caste where the caste is socially backward, but the creamy layer must be excluded, total reservation must not exceed 50% save in extraordinary situations, and reservation is confined to initial appointment, not promotion. Certain

04Facts

What actually happened

  • 1953–55. First Backward Classes Commission (Kaka Kalelkar). Report not acted upon. Strong
  • 1 January 1979. Second Backward Classes Commission appointed under Article 340, chaired by B.P. Mandal. Strong
  • December 1980. Report: identified 3,743 castes as backward, estimated them at about 52% of the population, recommended 27% reservation. Strong
  • 13 August 1990. Office Memorandum (V.P. Singh Government): 27% of vacancies in civil posts and services under the Government of India, filled by direct recruitment, reserved for SEBCs. Certain
  • Reaction. Widespread agitation. Writ petitions filed; the Court stayed the OM. Certain
  • 25 September 1991. Second OM (P.V. Narasimha Rao Government): within the 27%, preference to the poorer sections; and 10% of posts for "other economically backward sections" not covered by any existing scheme. Certain
  • Reference. A five-judge Bench referred the matter to nine Judges to settle the law finally. Strong

05Procedural History

How it reached the Court

  1. Writ petitions under Article 32 led by Indra Sawhney, an advocate. Strong
  2. Interim stay of the first OM.
  3. Second OM issued during pendency and brought under challenge.
  4. Reference to a nine-judge Bench, given earlier conflicting decisions of smaller Benches (Balaji, Devadasan, Thomas, Vasanth Kumar). Strong
  5. Jeevan Reddy J. framed the questions for decision; six opinions were delivered on 16 November 1992. Strong

08Ratio Decidendi

The rules, one by one

1 — Article 16(4) and 16(1)

Article 16(4) is not an exception but an emphatic statement of what Article 16(1) already allows. State of Kerala v. N.M. Thomas (1976) approved on this; the "exception" view of Balaji and Devadasan not followed. Certain

2 — Identifying the backward class

"Class" in Article 16(4) means social class. In India caste is often a social class; identification can begin with castes and extend to occupational groups and communities among non-Hindus. The test is social backwardness, which generally produces educational and economic backwardness. No fixed procedure is prescribed. Certain

3 — Economic criterion alone is impermissible

A backward class cannot be identified only and exclusively with reference to economic criteria. Hence the 10% for "other economically backward sections" fails. Certain

4 — Creamy layer

Socially advanced members of a backward class cease to be backward and must be excluded. Economic criteria may be used as an indicator of social advancement for that purpose. Certain

5 — The 50% rule

Reservations under Article 16(4) should not exceed 50% of the appointments in a year. Extraordinary situations — for example far-flung and remote areas — may justify some relaxation, with extreme caution and a special case made out. The carry-forward rule is valid so long as the year's total stays within 50%; Devadasan overruled to that extent. Certain

6 — No reservation in promotion

Article 16(4) covers initial appointment only. The ruling is prospective: existing promotion reservations may continue for five years from the date of judgment. Certain

7 — Backward and more backward

Sub-classification among backward classes is permissible, so that the more backward are not crowded out. Certain

8 — Executive order

Reservation need not be by law made by the legislature; an executive order is a "provision". Certain

9 — Posts where reservation is inadvisable

For certain services and posts — where merit alone counts — reservation may not be advisable; the majority instanced technical posts in research and defence, super-specialities in medicine, professors, pilots and the like. Strong

09Obiter & Observations

What is not binding

  • The majority expressly did not rule on the correctness of the Mandal Commission's findings or its list. Strong
  • The list of posts where reservation is inadvisable is guidance to the Government, not a judicial prohibition. Strong
  • Views in individual opinions on reservation for the poor among forward classes as a matter of policy.

12Precedent Chain

Where the case sits in the line

Later developments — not part of this judgment

  • National Commission for Backward Classes Act, 1993 — the permanent body the Court directed. Given constitutional status by the 102nd Amendment, 2018 (Article 338B). Strong
  • Creamy-layer criteria — issued by OM of 8 September 1993. Strong
  • Jaishri Laxmanrao Patil v. Chief Minister (2021) — five Judges refused to revisit the 50% rule and struck down the Maratha quota. Strong
  • Janhit Abhiyan v. Union of India (2022) — 103rd Amendment (EWS, 10%) upheld 3:2; the 50% ceiling held not to be inviolable against an amendment of this kind. Strong
  • State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (2024) — seven Judges, 6:1: Scheduled Castes may be sub-classified. Strong

13Dissent & Concurring Opinions

Dissenting and concurring opinions

  • Pandian J. (concurring). Upheld the 27% OM. Differed on two points: no numerical ceiling can be laid down, and no section should be excluded as creamy layer until a commission examines the matter. Would strike down the "poorer sections" preference in the second OM. Certain
  • Sawant J. (concurring). Agreed with the majority; promotion reservations unconstitutional but relaxations and concessions permissible; economic sub-classification rejected. Certain
  • Thommen J. (dissenting). Reservation is a remedy for past discrimination, to stay well below 50%; favoured periodic review by a permanent authority. Certain
  • Kuldip Singh J. (dissenting). "Class" cannot be read as "caste"; identification by caste collectivities is impermissible; reservation above 50% invalid. Certain
  • Sahai J. (dissenting). Caste-based identification contrary to the Constitution; creamy layer must be excluded by income, property or status. Certain

Trap

On promotions, all the judges who addressed the point were against reservation; Ahmadi J. declined to answer it. Certain Do not write "6:3" for that holding.

14Final Holding

The operative result

  • OM of 13 August 1990 — valid and enforceable, subject to exclusion of the creamy layer. Certain
  • OM of 25 September 1991, clause giving preference to "poorer sections" — valid if read as distinguishing the more backward from the backward on social, not purely economic, grounds. Certain
  • OM of 25 September 1991, clause reserving 10% for other economically backward sections — invalid and struck down. Certain

15Directions & Relief

What was ordered

  1. Union, States and Union Territories to constitute, within four months, a permanent body to examine requests for inclusion in and complaints of over- or under-inclusion in the backward class lists; its advice ordinarily binding. Strong
  2. Government of India to specify, within four months, the socio-economic criteria for excluding the creamy layer; implementation of the 27% to follow, with that exclusion. Strong
  3. Reservation in promotion already in force to continue for five years. Certain
  4. Any challenge to the creamy-layer criteria to be brought only before the Supreme Court. Strong

16AOR-Specific Importance

Why an AOR candidate must own this case

  • Every reservation brief starts here. A petition must say which of the nine rules is breached — ceiling, creamy layer, identification, promotion.
  • Pleading data. After Nagaraj and Jarnail Singh, inadequacy of representation must be shown by quantifiable data; the root of that requirement is the wording of Article 16(4) as explained here.
  • Reading a plural judgment. Six opinions; the binding propositions are those of Jeevan Reddy J. where Pandian and Sawant JJ. agree, or where any five concur.
  • Prospective overruling in service law. The five-year saving for promotions is a model.
  • Links on the list. Jarnail Singh (49), Pramati (33), and the EWS line.

Answer in two or three lines

Nine Judges; 6:3.

Q2Which Office Memorandum was struck down and to what extent?

The OM of 25 September 1991, to the extent it reserved 10% of posts for other economically backward sections.

Q3What is the "creamy layer"?

The socially advanced members of a backward class, who must be excluded from the benefit of reservation.

Q4What is the ceiling on reservation?

50% of appointments in a year, to be exceeded only in extraordinary situations.

Q5Does Article 16(4) extend to promotions?

No, per Indra Sawhney. Parliament later inserted Article 16(4A) for SCs and STs.

Article 340.

185-Mark Questions

Answer in about 150 words

Q1Is Article 16(4) an exception to Article 16(1)?

No. The majority held that Article 16(1) itself permits reasonable classification, and Article 16(4) is an instance of such classification — an emphatic way of stating what clause (1) already allows. It approved N.M. Thomas on this point and departed from the "exception" view in Balaji and Devadasan. Two consequences follow. First, clause (4) is not to be read narrowly as exceptions are. Second, clause (4) is exhaustive of reservations in favour of backward classes, but clause (1) may in very exceptional situations support other kinds of reservation.

Q2Explain the 50% rule and its exception.

Reservations contemplated by Article 16(4) should not exceed 50% of the appointments, taking the year as the unit. The rule balances the claims of backward classes with the equality of others and the efficiency of administration. In extraordinary situations inherent in the diversity of the country — the Court instanced far-flung and remote areas whose population needs different treatment — some relaxation may be imperative, but extreme caution and a special case are required. The carry-forward of unfilled vacancies is valid if the total in a year does not cross 50%.

Q3Can caste be the basis for identifying a backward class?

Yes, as a starting point. A caste is a socially homogeneous class and, where it is socially backward, it is a backward class for Article 16(4). Identification is not confined to Hindus; occupational groups and communities of other religions may be included. The ultimate test is social backwardness, not caste as such, and the advanced among the caste are excluded.

Q4What happened to reservation in promotion after the judgment?

The Court confined Article 16(4) to initial appointment but let existing schemes run five years. Parliament inserted Article 16(4A) (77th Amendment, 1995), then 16(4B) and consequential seniority (81st and 85th Amendments). M. Nagaraj (2006) upheld them subject to conditions; Jarnail Singh (2018) modified those conditions.

19Long-Answer Questions

Structure for a 15–20 mark answer

Q1Critically examine the principles laid down in Indra Sawhney on reservation under Article 16(4).

Introduction. Mandal Commission; two OMs; nine Judges; 6:3. Nature of 16(4). Not an exception; exhaustive for backward classes. Identification. Caste as class; social backwardness; not economic alone; creamy layer. Extent. 50%; year as unit; carry-forward; extraordinary situations. Field. Initial appointment; not promotion; inadvisable posts. Form. Executive order; sub-classification. Result. 27% upheld; 10% struck down; directions. Dissents. Thommen, Kuldip Singh, Sahai JJ. After. Arts. 16(4A), 16(4B); Nagaraj; Jarnail Singh; 103rd Amendment; Janhit Abhiyan; Davinder Singh. Evaluation. Has the 50% rule held? Has creamy layer worked? Conclusion.

Q2Trace the law on the ceiling on reservations from Balaji to Janhit Abhiyan.

Balaji (1963) — generally less than 50%. Thomas (1976) — doubts. Indra Sawhney (1992) — 50% as rule. Tamil Nadu's 69% and the Ninth Schedule; I.R. Coelho. Nagaraj (2006) — ceiling as constitutional requirement. Jaishri Patil (2021) — reaffirmed. Janhit Abhiyan (2022) — EWS 10% over and above upheld. Conclusion — rule for Article 16(4) classes; flexible against constitutional amendment.

22Top 10 Exam Traps

Ten ways to lose marks

  1. Saying the Court struck down the Mandal reservation.
  2. Calling the 50% limit absolute — it has an express exception.
  3. Saying caste is class — it can be, if socially backward.
  4. Writing that the judgment was unanimous.
  5. Stating that creamy layer was applied to SCs and STs here.
  6. Forgetting the five-year saving for promotions.
  7. Treating the judgment as having approved the Mandal report — it did not examine its correctness.
  8. Ignoring the later amendments when asked about the present law.
  9. Saying reservation must be made by legislation.
  10. Mixing the dates and contents of the two OMs.
