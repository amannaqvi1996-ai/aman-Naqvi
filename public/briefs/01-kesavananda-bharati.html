AOR Paper IVLeading Case 1 of 86Master Brief

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala

Thirteen judges, eleven opinions, a 7:6 split — and one sentence that has governed every constitutional amendment since: Parliament may amend any part of the Constitution, but not so as to alter its basic structure.

(1973) 4 SCC 225AIR 1973 SC 1461[1973] Supp. SCR 124 April 197313 Judges · 7:6

Read this first — the answer is in the count, not the prose

There is no single majority judgment. Eleven opinions were delivered. The law of the case is the short "View by the Majority" signed by nine of the thirteen judges, and the arithmetic that produces it. Six judges found implied limits on the amending power; six found none; Khanna J. stood alone in the middle — no implied limitations, but the word "amend" cannot reach the basic structure. Learn the three groups (6 – 1 – 6) and every holding follows.

On sources. No judgment file was uploaded. I worked from the Supreme Court's own head-notes volume for Paper IV and the settled reported position. Every proposition carries a confidence tag; §30 lists what I could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Trigger. The head of the Edneer Mutt in Kerala challenged the Kerala Land Reforms legislation, which restricted the Mutt's holding and management of its property.
  2. Escalation. While the petition was pending Parliament passed the 24th, 25th and 29th Amendments; the challenge was widened to all three.
  3. Issue. Is the amending power under Article 368 unlimited — and was Golak Nath (1967), which put Part III beyond amendment, correct?
  4. Rule. Golak Nath is overruled: an amendment is not "law" under Article 13(2). Every provision, including fundamental rights, can be amended.
  5. Ratio. But Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution (7:6).
  6. Holding. 24th Amendment valid. 25th: section 2 valid; section 3 (Article 31C) valid in its first part, invalid in its second part (the clause shutting out judicial review). 29th valid.
  7. Significance. The source of the basic structure doctrine — the measure applied in Indira Gandhi, Minerva Mills, Waman Rao, Bommai, L. Chandra Kumar, I.R. Coelho and the NJAC case.

03One-Line Ratio

The sentence to reproduce

The ratio in one line

Article 368 empowers Parliament to amend any provision of the Constitution, including the fundamental rights, but it does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution. Certain

Write it in two halves. The first half overrules Golak Nath. The second half is the limitation. A candidate who writes only the second half loses the marks for the first.

04Facts

What actually happened

  • The petitioner. Kesavananda Bharati was the head of the Edneer Mutt, a Hindu religious institution in Kerala (Kasaragod district). Strong
  • The grievance. Kerala's land reform legislation — the Kerala Land Reforms Act, 1963, as amended in 1969 — imposed restrictions that affected the Mutt's land. He petitioned under Article 32 in 1970, pleading Articles 14, 19(1)(f), 25, 26 and 31. Strong
  • The background. In Golak Nath (1967) an eleven-judge Bench had held, 6:5, that Parliament could not amend Part III so as to take away or abridge fundamental rights. Parliament responded.
  • The 24th Amendment (1971). Amended Articles 13 and 368 to declare that Parliament may, in exercise of constituent power, amend any provision, and that Article 13 does not apply to such amendments.
  • The 25th Amendment (1971). Section 2 replaced "compensation" in Article 31(2) with "amount" and inserted Article 31(2B). Section 3 inserted Article 31C: laws giving effect to Article 39(b) and (c) could not be challenged under Articles 14, 19 or 31, and a declaration to that effect could not be questioned in court.
  • The 29th Amendment (1972). Placed the two Kerala Land Reforms amending Acts of 1969 and 1971 in the Ninth Schedule.
  • The enlargement. The petition was amended to challenge all three Amendments. Because Golak Nath was by eleven Judges, a Bench of thirteen was constituted.
  • The hearing. Arguments ran for about five months, from late October 1972 to March 1973. Strong N.A. Palkhivala led for the petitioners; H.M. Seervai and the Attorney-General Niren De led for the State and the Union. Strong

05Procedural History

How it reached thirteen Judges

  1. 1970. Writ petition filed directly in the Supreme Court under Article 32. There was no High Court round.
  2. 1971–72. The three Amendments were enacted during pendency and added to the challenge.
  3. Reference. Since the validity of the Amendments depended on whether Golak Nath was right, and Golak Nath was an eleven-judge decision, only a larger Bench could reconsider it.
  4. 24 April 1973. Eleven opinions delivered. Nine Judges signed a short statement headed "View by the Majority" setting out six conclusions. Certain
  5. After judgment. The individual petitions were directed to be placed before a Constitution Bench to decide the validity of the Kerala Acts in accordance with the law declared. Strong

Trap

This Bench did not decide whether the Kerala Land Reforms Acts were valid. It decided the power question and remitted the rest.

Later development — keep separate

The right to property (Arts. 19(1)(f) and 31) was removed from Part III by the 44th Amendment, 1978; it survives as Article 300A. Article 31C was widened by the 42nd Amendment and that widening was struck down in Minerva Mills (1980). In 2024 a nine-judge Bench in Property Owners Association v. State of Maharashtra held that Article 31C, to the extent upheld in Kesavananda, remains in force. Strong

08Ratio Decidendi

Six conclusions, and the arithmetic behind them

The "View by the Majority", signed by nine Judges, records: Certain

  1. Golak Nath is overruled.
  2. Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution.
  3. The 24th Amendment is valid.
  4. Section 2(a) and 2(b) of the 25th Amendment is valid.
  5. The first part of section 3 of the 25th Amendment (Article 31C) is valid. The second part — that no law containing a declaration that it gives effect to such policy shall be questioned in any court on the ground that it does not — is invalid.
  6. The 29th Amendment is valid.

Proposition 1 — an amendment is not "law" under Article 13(2)

Rule. The power to amend is a constituent power, distinct from ordinary legislative power. Reasoning. Article 13(2) controls laws made under the Constitution, not changes to the Constitution itself. Result. Golak Nath overruled; Shankari Prasad and Sajjan Singh restored on this point. Certain

Proposition 2 — the basic structure limitation

Rule. The power to "amend" does not include the power to abrogate or destroy the Constitution's identity. Reasoning (the six). Limitations are implied from the scheme of the Constitution, the Preamble and the nature of the document. Reasoning (Khanna J.). No implied limitations; but the word "amendment" itself means the old Constitution survives without loss of identity — one cannot amend a thing out of existence. Result. Seven Judges agree on the limitation though by two routes. Certain

Proposition 3 — fundamental rights can be abridged

The majority did not hold Part III unamendable. Right to property, specifically, was held by Khanna J. not to be part of the basic structure. Certain

Proposition 4 — judicial review cannot be shut out by declaration

The second part of Article 31C was struck down because it left it to the legislature to certify its own law and barred the Court from checking whether the law really served Article 39(b) or (c). Certain

09Obiter & Observations

What is not binding

  • The lists of basic features are illustrative. No list commanded seven votes. Certain
  • Sikri C.J.'s wider view that fundamental rights cannot be abrogated (only reasonably abridged) is the view of six, not seven. Khanna J. did not join it. Certain
  • The minority's reasoning — that the amending power is plenary and may reach every provision — is not law, though parts of it (constituent power is distinct from legislative power) are common ground.
  • Preamble. The observation in In re Berubari Union (1960) that the Preamble is not part of the Constitution was disapproved; the Preamble is part of the Constitution and can itself be amended, subject to the basic structure. Certain

10Judicial Reasoning

How the two sides reasoned

StepMajority sideMinority side
Nature of powerConstituent, but derived from the Constitution and so bounded by it.Constituent and plenary; nothing is beyond it.
Meaning of "amend"To improve or change while preserving identity.Includes add, vary, repeal — any change.
PreambleA guide to essential features.Cannot control clear operative words.
FearAn unlimited power lets a transient majority destroy democracy lawfully.An unamendable core invites revolution; trust the people's representatives.
Judicial reviewEssential — someone must decide whether the limit is crossed.Courts cannot sit over the wisdom of amendments.

Khanna J.'s position decided the case. He rejected implied limitations and natural-rights reasoning, upheld the amendability of fundamental rights, and still held that the power to amend does not include the power to abrogate the Constitution or alter its basic structure. In Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) he clarified that he had not held fundamental rights to be outside the basic structure — only that the right to property was. Strong

12Precedent Chain

Where the case sits in the line

Later developments — not part of this judgment

  • November 1975. A thirteen-judge Bench assembled under Ray C.J. to reconsider Kesavananda; it was dissolved after two days of hearing without any order on the merits. Strong
  • 42nd Amendment, 1976. Inserted Article 368(4) and (5) to bar judicial review of amendments. Struck down in Minerva Mills.
  • NJAC case (2015). The 99th Amendment was struck down for violating judicial independence — a basic feature.

GroupJudgesPosition

On Article 31C the count is different. The six of the minority upheld all of it; Khanna J. and Jaganmohan Reddy J. upheld the first part only; five struck all of it down. So the first part survives 8:5, and the second part falls 7:6. Strong

Aftermath. Sikri C.J. retired the day after judgment. A.N. Ray J., fourth in seniority and in the minority, was appointed Chief Justice over Shelat, Hegde and Grover JJ., who resigned. Strong This is context, not law — do not build an answer on it.

14Final Holding

The operative result

  • Golak Nath overruled. Certain
  • Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework. Certain
  • 24th Amendment — valid. Certain
  • 25th Amendment, section 2 — valid; the "amount" need not be market value but cannot be illusory or fixed on irrelevant principles (per the judges who addressed it). Strong
  • 25th Amendment, section 3 — first part of Article 31C valid; declaration clause invalid. Certain
  • 29th Amendment — valid; whether the protected Acts damage the basic structure remains open when they are challenged. Strong

15Directions & Relief

What was ordered

  • The Constitution Bench was to determine the validity of the Kerala Land Reforms amending Acts in accordance with the law laid down. Strong
  • No order as to costs up to that stage. Strong
  • No declaration was made on the land-reform legislation itself.

Not established from the judgment: any finding on the Mutt's actual holdings or the application of Articles 25 and 26 to them.

16AOR-Specific Importance

Why an AOR candidate must own this case

  • It is the first case on the list and the foundation for at least a dozen others on it — Minerva Mills, Kihoto Hollohan, Indra Sawhney, Bommai, L. Chandra Kumar, I.R. Coelho, the NJAC case, Puttaswamy.
  • Drafting use. Any writ petition challenging a constitutional amendment must plead a specific basic feature and show that the amendment damages or destroys it. A bare plea of unreasonableness fails.
  • Test to apply. Identity test: does the Constitution after the amendment remain the same Constitution? Later refined in M. Nagaraj (2006) into the "width" and "identity" tests. Strong
  • Reading plural opinions. The case is the best training in extracting a ratio from fragmented opinions — a skill Paper IV rewards.
  • Bench strength rule. A decision can be reconsidered only by a larger Bench — why thirteen sat here.

172-Mark Questions

Answer in two or three lines

Q1What was overruled in Kesavananda Bharati?

I.C. Golak Nath v. State of Punjab (1967), which had held that Parliament could not amend Part III so as to take away or abridge fundamental rights.

Q2State the bench strength and the vote.

Thirteen Judges; 7:6 on the basic structure limitation.

Q3Which part of Article 31C was struck down?

The second part — the clause that a declaration that a law gives effect to the policy in Article 39(b) or (c) cannot be questioned in any court.

Q4Is the Preamble part of the Constitution?

Yes. The contrary observation in In re Berubari Union was disapproved. It can be amended subject to the basic structure.

Q5Name the five basic features listed by Sikri C.J.

Supremacy of the Constitution; republican and democratic form of government; secular character; separation of powers; federal character.

Q6What did the 24th Amendment do?

It amended Articles 13 and 368 to declare that Parliament may amend any provision in exercise of constituent power and that Article 13 does not apply to amendments.

185-Mark Questions

Answer in about 150 words

Q1Explain why Khanna J.'s opinion is treated as decisive.

Six Judges held that the amending power is subject to implied limitations; six held it is unlimited. Khanna J. agreed with the second group that there are no implied limitations and that fundamental rights, including property, can be abridged. He nevertheless held that the word "amendment" presupposes that the Constitution continues with its identity intact, so the power cannot be used to abrogate the Constitution or alter its basic structure or framework. That gave seven votes for the limitation, on the narrowest ground. The law of the case is therefore his formulation, not the wider view of Sikri C.J. and others that the essence of fundamental rights is unamendable.

Q2Distinguish Golak Nath from Kesavananda Bharati.
  • Golak Nath: 11 Judges, 6:5. Amendment is "law" under Art. 13(2). Part III cannot be abridged. Limitation located in Article 13. Applied prospectively.
  • Kesavananda: 13 Judges, 7:6. Amendment is not "law". Part III can be amended. Limitation located in the word "amend" in Article 368 — the basic structure. Protects the Constitution's identity, not any particular Article.
Q3What did the Court hold on the 25th Amendment?

Section 2, substituting "amount" for "compensation" in Article 31(2) and adding Article 31(2B), was upheld; adequacy of the amount is not justiciable. Section 3 inserted Article 31C. Its first part — immunity from Articles 14, 19 and 31 for laws giving effect to Article 39(b) and (c) — was upheld. Its second part, making the legislature's declaration conclusive, was struck down because it excluded judicial review.

Q4Why were thirteen Judges required?

The amendments could be valid only if Golak Nath was wrong. Golak Nath was decided by eleven Judges, and a Bench can be overruled only by a larger one. Hence thirteen.

19Long-Answer Questions

Structure for a 15–20 mark answer

Q1"Kesavananda Bharati neither gave Parliament what it wanted nor left Golak Nath standing." Discuss.

Introduction. Identify the case, Bench, date, vote. State the two-part ratio. Background. Shankari Prasad → Sajjan Singh → Golak Nath; the 24th, 25th, 29th Amendments as Parliament's reply. What Parliament won. Golak Nath overruled; amendment is constituent power; Part III amendable; 24th and 29th upheld; "amount" upheld; first part of 31C upheld. What Parliament lost. Basic structure limitation; declaration clause of 31C struck down; judicial review of amendments affirmed. How the ratio is derived. 6 – 1 – 6; Khanna J.; "View by the Majority" signed by nine. Content of the doctrine. Illustrative lists; identity test. Later history. Indira Gandhi; 42nd Amendment; Minerva Mills; Waman Rao; I.R. Coelho. Evaluation. Criticisms: no textual basis, uncertainty, judicial supremacy. Defence: prevents lawful destruction of the Constitution. Conclusion. The power to amend is wide but is a power under the Constitution, not over it.

Q2Trace the evolution of the basic structure doctrine from Sajjan Singh to I.R. Coelho.

Seed. Mudholkar J. in Sajjan Singh (1965) asks whether basic features can be changed. Detour. Golak Nath (1967) protects Part III through Article 13. Birth. Kesavananda (1973) — limitation in Article 368 itself. First use. Indira Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975) — free and fair elections, rule of law. Consolidation. Minerva Mills (1980) — limited amending power and judicial review are basic features; balance of Parts III and IV. Ninth Schedule. Waman Rao (1981) and I.R. Coelho (2007) — laws added after 24 April 1973 tested on basic structure. Extension. Bommai (secularism, federalism); L. Chandra Kumar (judicial review by High Courts); NJAC (judicial independence). Conclusion. From a doubt in a dissent to the central limit on constituent power.

22Top 10 Exam Traps

Ten ways to lose marks

  1. Writing that the decision was unanimous or that there is "a majority judgment".
  2. Saying Kesavananda held fundamental rights cannot be amended.
  3. Giving the vote as 9:4 because nine signed the summary.
  4. Putting Khanna J. in the minority — or forgetting he rejected implied limitations.
  5. Saying Article 31C was struck down entirely.
  6. Treating Sikri C.J.'s list as an exhaustive definition.
  7. Saying the Court decided the Kerala Land Reforms Acts.
  8. Confusing the 24th (power) with the 25th (property) Amendment.
  9. Citing the 42nd Amendment as if it were considered here — it came in 1976.
  10. Attributing "limited amending power is a basic feature" to this case; that sentence is Minerva Mills.

27Last-Night Revision Card

The section to read walking in — nothing else

Kesavananda Bharati v. State of Kerala, (1973) 4 SCC 225 — 24 April 1973 — 13 Judges, 7:6

Facts. Head of the Edneer Mutt, Kerala, challenged the Kerala Land Reforms Acts under Article 32. During pendency Parliament passed the 24th (power to amend), 25th ("amount"; Article 31C) and 29th (Ninth Schedule) Amendments, all brought under challenge. Thirteen Judges sat because Golak Nath was by eleven.

Issues. Is an amendment "law" under Article 13(2)? Is the power under Article 368 unlimited? Are the three Amendments valid?

Provisions. Arts. 13, 31, 31B, 31C, 368; Preamble; Ninth Schedule.

Ratio. (1) Golak Nath overruled — amendment is constituent power, not "law"; any provision including Part III may be amended. (2) Article 368 does not enable Parliament to alter the basic structure or framework of the Constitution.

How derived. Eleven opinions. 6 (implied limits) – 1 (Khanna J.) – 6 (plenary). "View by the Majority" signed by nine.

Holding. 24th valid. 25th s. 2 valid. Article 31C: first part valid, declaration clause invalid. 29th valid. Kerala Acts remitted to a Constitution Bench.

Basic features (illustrative). Sikri C.J.: supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism. Preamble is part of the Constitution.

Traps. Not unanimous. Not 9:4. Part III is amendable. 31C not wholly struck down. Property not basic (Khanna J.). Kerala Acts not decided.

Afterlife. Indira Gandhi (1975) → 42nd Amendment → Minerva Mills (1980) → Waman Rao (1981, cut-off 24.4.1973) → I.R. Coelho (2007).

One line. Parliament can amend any part of the Constitution, but not its basic structure.
