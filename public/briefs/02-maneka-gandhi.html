AOR Paper IVLeading Case No. 2Master Brief

Maneka Gandhi v. Union of India

The passport case that turned Article 21 from a formality into a promise — and made every future liberty argument in India possible.

(1978) 1 SCC 248 AIR 1978 SC 597 [1978] 2 SCR 621 25 January 1978 7 Judges

§The 60-Second Recall

If you read nothing else, read this

  1. Facts. Passport impounded by executive letter "in the interests of the general public" under s. 10(3)(c), Passports Act 1967. Reasons refused under the s. 10(5) proviso. No hearing, before or after. Writ under Art. 32.
  2. The break. A.K. Gopalan's "watertight compartments" theory is dead. Arts. 14, 19 and 21 are not mutually exclusive. A law taking away personal liberty must survive all three.
  3. Article 21 transformed. "Procedure established by law" does not mean any procedure a legislature enacts. It must be right, just and fair — not arbitrary, fanciful or oppressive. A procedure that is not fair is no procedure at all.
  4. Article 14 transformed. Art. 14 is not merely about classification; it strikes at arbitrariness itself. "Equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies."
  5. Natural justice. Audi alteram partem is read into a silent statute. Where urgency genuinely forbids a prior hearing, a post-decisional hearing must follow immediately.
  6. Outcome — the trap. Section 10(3)(c) was upheld, not struck down. And the Court passed no formal order quashing the impounding: the Attorney General's undertaking to hear her cured the defect, and the petition was disposed of without formal interference.

The two statutory provisions, in substance

Section 10(3)(c) empowers the passport authority to impound or revoke a passport if it deems it necessary to do so in the interests of the sovereignty and integrity of India, the security of India, friendly relations of India with any foreign country, or in the interests of the general public.

Section 10(5) requires the authority, on making such an order, to record in writing a brief statement of the reasons and to furnish a copy to the holder on demand — subject to a proviso permitting refusal where the authority is of opinion that furnishing reasons would not be in the interest of the same four heads.

Trap

Do not say the passport was revoked. It was impounded — a temporary deprivation of the document, which is precisely why the urgency argument and the post-decisional hearing device were available at all.

02Facts & Timeline

Six dates carry the whole case

The petitioner, a journalist, held a passport issued in 1976. In the aftermath of the Emergency the Shah Commission of Inquiry was sitting, and the Government wished to keep her within the jurisdiction. What it did not do — at any stage — was tell her why, or let her answer.

Why the facts matter doctrinally

The Court was not faced with a badly drafted statute. It was faced with a facially valid statute exercised in a procedurally empty way. That forced the Court to locate fairness somewhere other than the text — and it found it inside Article 21 itself.

03Issues

Frame them in this order and the answer writes itself

  1. Is the right to travel abroad part of "personal liberty" in Art. 21?
  2. Does "procedure established by law" in Art. 21 require the procedure to be fair, just and reasonable, or does any enacted procedure suffice?
  3. Are Arts. 14, 19 and 21 mutually exclusive (the Gopalan view), or do they overlap?
  4. Is s. 10(3)(c) void for excessive delegation / vagueness, or for offending Arts. 14, 19(1)(a), 19(1)(g) and 21?
  5. Where the statute is silent, must a hearing be given? Can a post-decisional hearing suffice?
  6. Does Art. 19(1)(a) operate extra-territorially?
  7. Was the refusal of reasons under the s. 10(5) proviso lawful on these facts?

04Gopalan → Cooper → Maneka

The single most examinable movement in Indian constitutional law

In A.K. Gopalan v. State of Madras, AIR 1950 SC 27 the Court held that each fundamental right occupied its own sealed field. A law of preventive detention was tested against Art. 21 (and Art. 22) alone; it never had to answer to Art. 19, because the object of the law was detention, not speech or movement.

That theory was already fatally wounded by R.C. Cooper v. Union of India, (1970) 1 SCC 248 (the Bank Nationalisation case), which held that what matters is not the object of the law but its direct and inevitable effect on the citizen's rights. Maneka took the last step and buried Gopalan on this point outright.

Black letter

"The law must therefore now be taken to be well settled that Article 21 does not exclude Article 19, and that even if there is a law prescribing a procedure for depriving a person of personal liberty and there is consequently no infringement of the fundamental right conferred by Article 21, such law, in so far as it abridges or takes away any fundamental right under Article 19, would have to meet the challenge of that Article."

Trap — the sharpest one in this case

The right to go abroad is not an Art. 19(1)(a) right. But the freedom of speech that Art. 19(1)(a) does guarantee has no territorial boundary. Both propositions are in the judgment and candidates constantly collapse them into one. Keep them apart: the first is about what the right is; the second is about where it can be exercised.

08Natural Justice

Where the statute says nothing, and where urgency says "not yet"

Two administrative-law propositions come out of Maneka and both are examined regularly. First, the collapse of the old distinction: for the purposes of natural justice there is no difference between a quasi-judicial and an administrative function — what matters is whether the action carries civil consequences. Second, the device of the post-decisional hearing.

Where the law stands now

The post-decisional hearing has been steadily cut back. In Swadeshi Cotton Mills v. Union of India, (1981) 1 SCC 664 the Court insisted that the exception operates only where a pre-decisional hearing is genuinely impossible. In K.I. Shephard v. Union of India, (1987) 4 SCC 431 it went further: a post-decisional hearing is ordinarily no substitute for a prior one, because once a decision is made the mind is closed. Cite Maneka for the principle, cite Shephard for its present limits.

If asked "what did the majority hold", the safe answer is Bhagwati, J. speaking for himself, Untwalia and Fazal Ali, JJ., with Beg C.J., Chandrachud and Krishna Iyer JJ. concurring in the result on separate reasoning. Do not describe the judgment as unanimous, and do not attribute Krishna Iyer's metaphors to Bhagwati.

11The Actual Order

The single most commonly misstated fact about this case

The Court did not strike down s. 10(3)(c), and it did not pass a formal order quashing the impounding.

During argument the Attorney General made a statement on behalf of the Government of India that it would consider any representation the petitioner might make in respect of the impounding of her passport, give her an opportunity in the matter, and deal with the representation expeditiously in accordance with law.

Bhagwati, J. held that this statement removed the vice from the order: the very defect complained of — the absence of a hearing — was cured by the undertaking. The Court therefore declined to interfere formally, and the petition was disposed of on that footing.

Trap

Several commercial digests and student summaries record the outcome as "petition allowed with costs; order quashed; passport directed to be returned." That is wrong, and it is exactly the kind of detail an examiner uses to separate candidates who read the judgment from candidates who read a summary. Write: no formal order was passed; the petition was disposed of in light of the Attorney General's statement.

The paradox worth a mark

The petitioner won nothing and everything. She obtained no writ; the Constitution obtained a new Article 21. If asked to comment critically, this is the point to make — the Court used a case it did not need to decide on relief to reset the whole of Part III, which is both its genius and the ground on which it has been criticised.

13Life After Maneka

14Proportionality

What has happened to Maneka's test since — and why it matters in 2026

Maneka gave us a standard — "fair, just and reasonable" — but not a structured test. Modern Article 21 and Article 19 review runs instead on proportionality, adopted in Modern Dental College v. State of M.P., (2016) 7 SCC 353 and settled in K.S. Puttaswamy, (2017) 10 SCC 1. Proportionality does not displace Maneka; it operationalises it.

Current law, in one line

Maneka remains good law and is cited constantly. What has changed is the method: the "fair, just and reasonable" standard is today applied through the four-limb proportionality enquiry — see Anuradha Bhasin v. Union of India, (2020) 3 SCC 637 — and Art. 14's arbitrariness limb has been extended to strike down legislation as manifestly arbitrary in Shayara Bano v. Union of India, (2017) 9 SCC 1.

15Twelve Traps

Read this list last thing before you walk in

Do not writeWrite instead
"Section 10(3)(c) was struck down."It was upheld — read down, with natural justice implied into it.
"The petition was allowed with costs."No formal order. Disposed of on the Attorney General's undertaking.
"The Court adopted American due process."It read fairness into "procedure established by law". The text of Art. 21 was never amended, and the framers' rejection of "due process" was not overturned.
"Maneka overruled ADM Jabalpur."Puttaswamy (2017) did that, expressly. Maneka made it untenable.
"Gopalan was overruled entirely."Overruled on exclusivity. Its Art. 22 preventive-detention holding stands.
"The right to travel abroad is under Art. 19(1)(a)."It is under Art. 21. Separately, Art. 19(1)(a) speech has no geographical limit.
"The passport was revoked."Impounded.
"The judgment was unanimous."Five opinions; Kailasam J. dissented in reasoning on extra-territoriality.
"A post-decisional hearing is always sufficient."Exceptional only. See K.I. Shephard (1987).
"Article 14 only prohibits unreasonable classification."After Royappa and Maneka it strikes at arbitrariness itself.
"The golden triangle is a phrase used in the judgment."It is later shorthand. The judgment speaks of Part III as an integral whole.
"It was a five-judge bench."Seven.

17Memory Hooks

Built for recall under time pressure, not for elegance

The case in seven words

Passport · No reasons · No hearing · Three gates.

The three gates — 21 → 19 → 14

Descending numbers, ascending abstraction: Fair procedure, Reasonable restriction, Non-arbitrary. FRN — "Fair, Reasonable, Not arbitrary."

The chain of authority

G-C-M-P — Gopalan sealed it, Cooper cracked it, Maneka opened it, Puttaswamy structured it. If you can recite those four names in order with their years (1950 · 1970 · 1978 · 2017) you can construct an answer on almost any Art. 21 question.

The disposition

"She won the law, not the writ."

The five opinions

B-B-C-K-K — Bhagwati (plurality), Beg, Chandrachud, Krishna Iyer (concurring), Kailasam (dissenting in reasoning).

18Question Drill

Six likely framings, with the skeleton each one wants

1. "Discuss how Maneka Gandhi altered the interpretation of Article 21."

Skeleton: Gopalan position → Cooper effect test → Maneka's two moves (wide reading of personal liberty; fairness requirement in "procedure established by law") → the three gates → consequences (the Art. 21 expansion line) → present position under proportionality. Close with the ratio sentence verbatim.

2. "'Equality and arbitrariness are sworn enemies.' Explain."

Skeleton: Traditional Art. 14 (classification + intelligible differentia + rational nexus) → Royappa's new dimension → Maneka applying it to constitutional review → Ajay Hasia, Shayara Bano (manifest arbitrariness applied to legislation) → note the criticism that arbitrariness is a standard without a structure, and that proportionality now supplies the structure.

3. Problem question: an authority acts under a statute silent as to hearing.

Skeleton: Identify civil consequences → natural justice presumptively applies (Maneka) → quasi-judicial/administrative distinction is irrelevant → is there express exclusion or necessary implication? → is there genuine urgency? → if yes, post-decisional hearing, but note Shephard's narrowing → conclude on the facts.

4. "The golden triangle. Comment."

Skeleton: Draw it. Name the three Articles and what each contributes → Maneka as its source → basic-structure resonance (Kesavananda, Minerva Mills) → note that the phrase is judicial shorthand, not the judgment's own language.

5. "Was Maneka Gandhi rightly decided?"

Skeleton (give both sides — the examiner wants balance): For — restored constitutional protection after the Emergency; made Part III coherent; enabled four decades of rights jurisprudence. Against — the Court effectively read in "due process" that the Constituent Assembly deliberately rejected; "fair, just and reasonable" was an unstructured standard inviting judicial subjectivity; and the Court decided sweeping questions in a case where it granted no relief. Conclude that the criticism has been substantially answered by the adoption of proportionality.

6. "State the ratio decidendi."

One sentence: A law depriving a person of personal liberty must not only prescribe a procedure, but that procedure must be right, just and fair — and the law must additionally satisfy Articles 14 and 19, which are not mutually exclusive of Article 21.

19Exam Hall

Strategy notes specific to Paper IV

  • Headnotes are supplied. For Paper IV, headnotes of all the listed leading cases are provided in the examination hall in bound volumes (and collected back afterwards). This changes what you should memorise: do not spend revision time on facts and paragraph numbers you can look up. Spend it on the things the headnote will not give you — the ratio in your own words, the doctrinal movement, the traps, and the ability to apply the principle to a new fact pattern.
  • Confirm the current list. The leading-cases list is revised by notification. Maneka Gandhi has sat at or near the top of it for years — check the live list on the Supreme Court's AOR Examination page when you plan your revision order, since the list is what defines the syllabus.
  • Write the citation once, correctly, early. (1978) 1 SCC 248. Getting a citation wrong in an AOR answer costs more than it does anywhere else — you are being examined as a prospective officer of the Court.
  • Structure beats volume. Facts (3 lines) → Issues (numbered) → Holding (numbered, matching the issues) → Ratio (one sentence, underlined) → Significance / present position. Examiners can find your marks in that shape.
  • Cross-paper leverage. This case earns marks in three papers, not one. Paper I (Practice & Procedure) — Art. 32 jurisdiction. Paper II (Drafting) — a writ petition under Art. 32 pleading violation of Arts. 14, 19 and 21 is a standard drafting question, and this case supplies the grounds. Paper IV — the leading case itself.

If you are drafting, not answering

A writ petition modelled on these facts pleads: (i) the impugned order deprives the petitioner of personal liberty under Art. 21 without a fair procedure; (ii) it has a direct and inevitable effect on rights under Art. 19(1) and is not saved by 19(2)–(6); (iii) it is arbitrary and so offends Art. 14; (iv) it was made without notice or hearing, contrary to natural justice; and (v) reasons were withheld without justification. Prayer: writ of certiorari quashing the order and mandamus directing its return.
