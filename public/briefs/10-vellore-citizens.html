What kind of judgment is this?

  • CERTAIN Landmark and leading judgment. Basis, not adjective: it is the first Indian decision to hold expressly that the Precautionary Principle and the Polluter Pays Principle are part of the environmental law of the country, and to hold that customary international law on the environment stands incorporated into domestic law. Both propositions are quoted in nearly every later Indian environmental judgment and are now reproduced in s. 20 of the National Green Tribunal Act, 2010.
  • CERTAIN Primarily doctrinal, with a heavy remedial/administrative component. The facts are local; the law is general.
  • CERTAIN Important for constitutional law — Article 21 read with Articles 47, 48-A, 51-A(g); the role of Directive Principles and Fundamental Duties in construing a fundamental right.
  • STRONG Procedurally important, but not an "AOR procedure" case. It is important for Article 32 PIL practice, continuing mandamus and the design of operative directions — not for filing, limitation, Registry or Vakalatnama questions.
  • DO NOT RELY It is not a Constitution Bench judgment. Anyone who writes "Constitution Bench" has lost the mark.

Part 02

If I Have Only 60 Seconds

The whole case in ten lines
  • Facts: More than 900 tanneries in five districts of Tamil Nadu discharged untreated effluent; the Palar river and the groundwater it feeds were ruined. 35,000 hectares of farmland became unfit for cultivation; 350 of 467 surveyed wells were polluted.
  • Petition: PIL under Article 32 by the Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum, filed 1991, heard through five years of interim orders.
  • Central tension: Tamil Nadu supplies about 80% of India's finished-leather exports; closing tanneries costs foreign exchange and jobs. Pollute or shut down?
  • Answer: Neither. Sustainable Development — development and ecology are not opposed; a balance is struck, industry continues but pays and remediates.
  • Rule (this is the case): The Precautionary Principle and the Polluter Pays Principle are essential features of sustainable development, are part of customary international law, and — not being contrary to municipal law — are part of the law of India.
  • Precautionary Principle, 3 limbs: anticipate–prevent–attack causes; lack of scientific certainty is no reason to postpone; onus of proof is on the developer/industrialist to show his act is environmentally benign.
  • Polluter Pays: absolute liability covers both compensation to victims and the cost of restoring the degraded environment. Remediation is part of sustainable development.
  • Domestic anchor: Art. 21 + Art. 47 + Art. 48-A + Art. 51-A(g) + the Water Act 1974, Air Act 1981 and EP Act 1986 — so the principles are Indian law even without the international law route.
  • Relief: An authority under s. 3(3) EP Act 1986 headed by a retired High Court judge, to assess ecological loss and individual compensation, recoverable as arrears of land revenue; Rs. 10,000 pollution fine per tannery into an Environment Protection Fund; CETPs or ETPs by a deadline or closure.
  • Institutional afterthought that gets asked: a "Green Bench" in the Madras High Court, and Rs. 50,000 costs to Mr. M.C. Mehta.

Part 03

One-Line Ratio

One-line ratio

The Precautionary Principle and the Polluter Pays Principle, being essential features of Sustainable Development and rules of customary international law not contrary to municipal law, are part of the environmental law of India; they are independently sustained by Article 21 read with Articles 47, 48-A and 51-A(g) and by the Water, Air and Environment (Protection) Acts, and their operation places the onus on the industrialist to prove his activity is environmentally benign and casts on him absolute liability both to compensate victims and to bear the cost of restoring the degraded environment.

EXAM MEMORY HOOK  →  "P³ + O³" — Palar river, Precaution, Polluter pays; Onus on the polluter, One authority under s. 3(3), One fund (Rs. 10,000 per tannery).

Do not over-simplify

"Polluter pays" in this judgment does not mean "pay a fee and pollute". The Court adopted the Bichhri formulation: the liability is absolute, and it is liability for restoration of the environment over and above compensation to victims. Equally, the Precautionary Principle is not merely "be careful" — its operative teeth are the reversal of the burden of proof.

Part 04

Facts

Material facts

  • CERTAIN Tanneries and other industries in Tamil Nadu were discharging untreated effluent into agricultural fields, roadsides, waterways and open lands, which ultimately reached the Palar river — the main source of water supply for the surrounding population.
  • CERTAIN More than 900 tanneries in five districts: North Arcot Ambedkar, Erode Periyar, Dindigul Anna, Tiruchirapalli and Chengai M.G.R.
  • CERTAIN In North Arcot Ambedkar district alone: 584 tanneries; 443 had applied to the Board for consent; only 33 had set up effluent treatment plants (position as on 9 January 1992).
  • CERTAIN 35,000 hectares of agricultural land had become partially or totally unfit for cultivation.
  • CERTAIN 350 out of 467 wells used for drinking and irrigation had been polluted.
  • STRONG The tanning process uses on the order of 170 types of chemicals, and roughly 35 litres of water per kilogram of finished leather; the effluent carried a large number of chemicals and acids. Treat the precise counts as approximations — different reports in the record give slightly different figures.
  • CERTAIN Villagers faced an acute shortage of drinking water; the Tamil Nadu Agricultural University Research Centre at Vellore and the Board's own material documented the damage.

Background facts (context, not the ratio)

  • The leather industry is a major foreign-exchange earner and employer; Tamil Nadu accounts for roughly 80% of India's export of finished leather. CERTAIN
  • The Tamil Nadu Pollution Control Board had been persuading tanneries to install pollution-control devices for about ten years without success; the Central Government agreed to a substantial subsidy for common effluent treatment plants (CETPs). CERTAIN (The exact subsidy percentages are not established from the judgment as reported.)
The examiner's favourite factual contrast

An industry that earns the country foreign exchange was destroying a river and 35,000 hectares of farmland. The Court expressly refused to treat this as a choice between the two — that refusal is the doorway to Sustainable Development. Write the contrast; it earns the mark.

Part 05

Procedural History

  1. Original proceeding: There was none below. The matter began in the Supreme Court as a writ petition under Article 32 filed in 1991 by the Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum, a registered body, in the public interest. CERTAIN
  2. Nature of jurisdiction: Original jurisdiction under Article 32 for enforcement of fundamental rights — here Article 21. It is not an appeal, not Article 136, not Article 131. CERTAIN
  3. Interim management: The Court did not decide and depart. It classified industries into statements/lists, gave time-bound directions to install treatment plants, ordered closures on default, suspended some closures, and appointed NEERI (National Environmental Engineering Research Institute) to report on the feasibility and performance of CETPs. CERTAIN
  4. NEERI reports: a report of 12 February 1996 recorded 30 identified CETP sites — 7 commissioned, 10 under construction, 13 proposed — with further reports in May and June 1996. STRONG
  5. Question before the Court at the final stage: not "is there pollution" (that was established) but what permanent institutional machinery should replace the Court's own supervision, and on what principles liability should be fixed. STRONG
Articles NOT engaged — say so, don't guess

Articles 131, 132, 133, 134, 136, 137 and 145 have no application here. Whether the Court invoked Article 142 is a live trap: on the reported text the directions are issued in exercise of Article 32 jurisdiction read with the constitutional and statutory scheme, and an express invocation of Article 142 is not established from the judgment. Do not write "the Court exercised its power under Article 142" unless the question itself supplies that premise. DO NOT RELY

Part 06

Issues

A. Constitutional issues

ISSUE 1Does the discharge of untreated industrial effluent that destroys drinking water sources and agricultural land violate a fundamental right?

Provision: Article 21, read with Articles 47, 48-A and 51-A(g).
Answer: Yes — the right to life includes the right to a wholesome environment; the Directive Principles and the Fundamental Duty inform and reinforce it, and give the State the constitutional mandate to protect the environment.
Importance: This is what makes an Article 32 petition maintainable at all.

ISSUE 2Must industry be shut down to protect the environment, or may it continue?

Answer: Neither extreme. Sustainable Development is a balancing concept: development and ecology are not opposed, and the traditional assumption that they are is no longer acceptable. Industry continues, subject to prevention, compensation and restoration.
Importance: The philosophical core; every later "development vs. environment" question begins here.

B. Issue of international law in a domestic court

ISSUE 3Are the Precautionary Principle and the Polluter Pays Principle part of Indian law, and if so how?

Answer: Yes, by two independent routes — (i) they are essential features of sustainable development and rules of customary international law, and a rule of customary international law not contrary to municipal law is deemed incorporated into domestic law; and (ii) even otherwise, they follow from the constitutional provisions and the three environmental statutes, which give effect to them.
Importance: The single most quoted holding of the case; also a standard "international law and municipal law" question.

C. Statutory / administrative issues

ISSUE 4What is the content of the power under s. 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, and had the Central Government discharged it?

Provision: Ss. 3(1), 3(2), 3(3) and 5, EP Act 1986.
Answer: The Act confers ample power, including the power under s. 3(3) to constitute an authority; the Central Government had not constituted one for the affected area despite the gravity of the situation. The Court therefore directed its constitution within a fixed date.
Importance: The remedial half of the judgment; the origin of the Loss of Ecology Authority.

ISSUE 5How should liability be quantified and recovered from polluting units?

Answer: By an expert authority applying the two principles, computing compensation under two heads — reversing the ecological damage, and payment to individuals — with the statement forwarded to the Collector/District Magistrate for recovery as arrears of land revenue, and closure of units that do not pay.
Importance: Shows how "polluter pays" is converted into an enforceable money remedy without a suit.

D. Procedural / institutional issue

ISSUE 6Should the Supreme Court continue to monitor the matter itself?

Answer: No. Monitoring was handed down to a Green Bench of the Madras High Court, the Court noting that such benches already functioned in Calcutta and other High Courts.
Importance: A rare, examinable example of the Supreme Court divesting itself of continuing supervision in a PIL.

NOTE No evidentiary or limitation issue arose; nor did any issue about the role or duties of an Advocate-on-Record. Saying so in an answer is better than inventing one.

Part 08

Ratio Decidendi

Proposition 1 — Sustainable Development is the governing standard CERTAIN

Rule: The traditional notion that development and ecology are opposed is no longer acceptable; "Sustainable Development" is the answer, and it is a balancing concept between ecology and development which has been accepted as part of customary international law.

Reasoning: Traced historically — Stockholm 1972, Brundtland 1987, Caring for the Earth 1991, Rio 1992 — the concept has moved from aspiration to accepted norm.

Application: The tanneries need not be shut down merely because they pollute, nor permitted merely because they earn foreign exchange.

Conclusion: Industry may continue subject to prevention, compensation and restoration.

Proposition 2 — The two principles are essential features of Sustainable Development CERTAIN

Rule: Of the salient principles culled from the Brundtland Report and other international documents — Inter-Generational Equity, Use and Conservation of Natural Resources, Environmental Protection, the Precautionary Principle, the Polluter Pays Principle, the Obligation to Assist and Cooperate, Eradication of Poverty, and Financial Assistance to Developing Countries — the Precautionary Principle and the Polluter Pays Principle are essential features.

Conclusion: They therefore travel with sustainable development into Indian law.

Proposition 3 — Content of the Precautionary Principle in municipal law CERTAIN

  1. Environmental measures — by the State Government and the statutory authorities — must anticipate, prevent and attack the causes of environmental degradation.
  2. Where there are threats of serious and irreversible damage, lack of scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing measures to prevent environmental degradation.
  3. The onus of proof is on the actor or the developer/industrialist to show that his action is environmentally benign.

Learn these three limbs verbatim in substance. They are the highest-yield lines in the whole judgment.

Proposition 4 — Content of the Polluter Pays Principle CERTAIN

Rule: Adopting Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action v. Union of India, the absolute liability for harm to the environment extends not only to compensate the victims of pollution but also the cost of restoring the environmental degradation. Remediation of the damaged environment is part of the process of "Sustainable Development", and as such the polluter is liable to pay the cost both to the individual sufferers and for reversing the damaged ecology.

Proposition 5 — Customary international law is part of Indian law if not contrary to municipal law CERTAIN

Rule: "Even otherwise, once these principles are accepted as part of the Customary International Law there would be no difficulty in accepting them as part of the domestic law. It is almost an accepted proposition of law that the rules of Customary International Law which are not contrary to the municipal law shall be deemed to have been incorporated in the domestic law and shall be followed by the courts of law."

Support: ADM Jabalpur v. Shivakant Shukla; Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin; Gramophone Company of India Ltd. v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey.

Proposition 6 — Domestic anchoring is independent and sufficient CERTAIN

Rule: The constitutional provisions (Arts. 21, 47, 48-A, 51-A(g)) and the three statutes make the two principles part of the environmental law of India in their own right.

Why this matters: it insulates the holding from any objection that international law cannot be applied directly by an Indian court.

Proposition 7 — Power under s. 3(3) EP Act is a power coupled with a duty STRONG

Rule / application: Given the scale of the damage, the Central Government could not leave the s. 3(3) power unexercised; the Court directed constitution of an authority by a fixed date and conferred on it the s. 5 powers.

Classified as STRONG rather than CERTAIN because the Court's reasoning is remedial and fact-driven; the general "power coupled with duty" formulation is drawn from the earlier Enviro-Legal Action line rather than laid down as a fresh rule here.

Part 10

Judicial Reasoning

The chain the Court actually walked — reproduce this chain and a long-answer question is more than half written.

  1. Facts establish irreversible harm. Untreated effluent → Palar river and groundwater → 35,000 hectares unfit, 350 of 467 wells polluted, acute drinking-water shortage. Harm is not speculative.
  2. Locate the right. Article 21 includes the right to a wholesome environment and unpolluted water; Articles 47 and 48-A cast the duty on the State, Article 51-A(g) on the citizen. The petition is therefore properly under Article 32.
  3. Confront the counter-interest. Foreign exchange and employment are real, but they do not license destruction of a river.
  4. Resolve by concept. Sustainable Development dissolves the supposed opposition: not "development or ecology", but development that does not compromise future generations.
  5. Extract the operative principles. Of the salient principles of sustainable development, two are essential and workable in a court: precaution and polluter pays.
  6. Give them a legal source — twice over. Customary international law (incorporated because not contrary to municipal law) and, "even otherwise", the Constitution plus the three statutes.
  7. Give precaution teeth. Reverse the burden: the industrialist must prove his activity is benign; uncertainty operates against him, not against the environment.
  8. Give polluter pays a measure. Absolute liability, following Enviro-Legal Action, covering victim compensation and the cost of ecological restoration.
  9. Find the statutory vehicle. The EP Act 1986 already supplies it — s. 3(3) to constitute an authority, s. 5 to arm it with directions including closure.
  10. Make the money recoverable without a suit. Assessment by the authority → statement to the Collector → recovery as arrears of land revenue → closure for non-payment.
  11. Exit. Hand continuing supervision to a Green Bench of the Madras High Court instead of retaining it in the Supreme Court.

Dissent and Concurring Opinions

Nothing to learn — and that is worth a mark

There is no dissent and no separate concurring opinion. The judgment is a unanimous judgment of three Judges, delivered by Kuldip Singh, J., for himself, Faizan Uddin, J. and K. Venkataswami, J. CERTAIN

Part 14

Final Holding

Held
  1. The right to life under Article 21 includes the right to a wholesome environment and to unpolluted water; Articles 47, 48-A and 51-A(g) reinforce the constitutional mandate to protect and improve the environment.
  2. Sustainable Development — a balancing concept between ecology and development — has been accepted as part of customary international law and is the governing standard; the traditional opposition between development and ecology is no longer acceptable.
  3. The Precautionary Principle and the Polluter Pays Principle are essential features of Sustainable Development and are part of the environmental law of India.
  4. The Precautionary Principle, in the municipal context, requires anticipation, prevention and attack on the causes of degradation; forbids treating scientific uncertainty as a reason to postpone action against threats of serious and irreversible damage; and places the onus of proof on the developer/industrialist.
  5. The Polluter Pays Principle imposes absolute liability extending to compensation of victims and the cost of restoring the degraded environment; remediation is part of sustainable development.
  6. Rules of customary international law not contrary to municipal law are deemed incorporated in domestic law and shall be followed by the courts.
  7. The tanneries, though economically important, cannot be permitted to destroy the ecology, degrade the environment or pose a health hazard; they must operate with treatment facilities or not at all.
  8. The Central Government must exercise its power under s. 3(3) of the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 to constitute an authority to give effect to these principles in the affected districts.
Result

Writ petition allowed. An eleven-point operative order was passed; no closure of the industry as a whole was ordered, but closure was directed for units failing to comply within the stipulated dates. Continuing supervision was transferred to a Green Bench of the Madras High Court. Costs of Rs. 50,000 were awarded to the petitioner's counsel, Mr. M.C. Mehta, payable by the State of Tamil Nadu.

Part 16

AOR-Specific Importance

Switching to Advocate-on-Record mode: what in this judgment is actually usable in Practice & Procedure and Professional Ethics answers, and what is not.

Genuinely relevant

  • Article 32 as original jurisdiction. The Supreme Court is the court of first instance; no exhaustion of alternative remedies was insisted on where a fundamental right and a continuing public injury were involved. CERTAIN
  • Locus standi in PIL. A registered welfare forum with no personal injury maintained the petition on behalf of affected residents; representative standing is assumed without demur. STRONG — the Court did not separately discuss locus, which itself tells you it was settled by 1991.
  • Continuing mandamus. A model illustration: the petition was kept pending for five years; industries classified into lists; time-bound compliance ordered; closures directed and suspended; an expert body (NEERI) appointed to report to the Court. Cite this whenever asked how the Supreme Court supervises compliance in PIL.
  • Delegation of supervision. The Court's exit strategy — a statutory authority for adjudication of compensation plus a High Court Green Bench for continuing supervision — is the examinable answer to "should the Supreme Court monitor a PIL forever?"
  • Fashioning relief beyond the pleadings. Fines, a fund, an assessment authority, revenue recovery and relocation — none of which any statute expressly commanded the Court to order — show the width of the Article 32 remedial power.
  • Recovery machinery. Compensation assessed by an authority and recovered as arrears of land revenue, with closure as the sanction — an alternative to a civil suit, and a favourite short-answer point.
  • Costs. Rs. 50,000 awarded to counsel in a PIL, payable by the State — a concrete instance of costs in public interest proceedings.
  • Expert assistance to the Court. NEERI's role prefigures the Court's modern practice of committees and expert reports; relevant to how an AOR places technical material before the Court.
  • Standard of proof in environmental matters. The reversal of the burden onto the polluter is a procedural holding with substantive force — the single most useful cross-over point between Paper I and Paper IV.
Not in this judgment — do not claim otherwise

This case decides nothing about: filing procedure, limitation or condonation of delay, court fees, Registry objections, defective petitions, service of notice, affidavits, Vakalatnama, the duties or accountability of an Advocate-on-Record, Order XV/XXI of the Supreme Court Rules, review or curative jurisdiction, or Article 136 leave. If asked "what does Vellore lay down on AOR duties?", the honest and correct answer is that it lays down nothing; its procedural value is confined to Article 32 PIL practice.

Part 18

5-Mark Questions

1. State the Precautionary Principle as explained in Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum.

Model answer. In the context of municipal law the Court explained the Precautionary Principle as meaning: (i) environmental measures, by the State Government and the statutory authorities, must anticipate, prevent and attack the causes of environmental degradation; (ii) where there are threats of serious and irreversible damage, lack of scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing measures to prevent environmental degradation; and (iii) the onus of proof is on the actor or the developer/industrialist to show that his action is environmentally benign. The principle is an essential feature of Sustainable Development and, being a rule of customary international law not contrary to municipal law, is part of the environmental law of India, sustained independently by Articles 21, 47, 48-A and 51-A(g) and the Water, Air and EP Acts.

Essential points: three limbs, verbatim in substance · "serious and irreversible" · onus reversal · status in Indian law. Common mistake: giving only the "better safe than sorry" idea and omitting the burden of proof — that third limb is where the marks are.

2. Explain the Polluter Pays Principle as applied in this case.

Model answer. Adopting Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action, the Court held that the absolute liability for harm to the environment extends not only to compensating the victims of pollution but also to the cost of restoring the environmental degradation; remediation of the damaged environment is itself part of the process of Sustainable Development. Consequently, the polluter is liable to pay both to the individual sufferers and for reversing the damaged ecology. The Court operationalised the principle by directing an authority under s. 3(3) of the EP Act, 1986 to assess compensation under two heads — reversal of ecology and payment to individuals — recoverable through the Collector as arrears of land revenue, with closure as the sanction for non-payment, and by imposing a Rs. 10,000 pollution fine on each tannery payable into an Environment Protection Fund. Liability was expressly retrospective: installing a treatment plant later does not absolve a unit of liability for earlier pollution.

Essential points: absolute liability · two heads · remediation as part of sustainable development · enforcement machinery. Common mistake: describing it as a "pay-and-pollute" licence, or as fault-based liability.

3. How did the Court hold customary international law to be part of Indian law?

Model answer. The Court held that once the Precautionary and Polluter Pays Principles are accepted as part of customary international law, there is no difficulty in accepting them as part of the domestic law: it is an accepted proposition that rules of customary international law which are not contrary to municipal law shall be deemed to have been incorporated into domestic law and shall be followed by the courts. It relied on Gramophone Company of India Ltd. v. Birendra Bahadur Pandey, Jolly George Varghese v. Bank of Cochin and ADM Jabalpur v. Shivakant Shukla. Crucially, the Court did not rest on this route alone: "even otherwise", the principles are part of Indian environmental law by force of Articles 21, 47, 48-A and 51-A(g) and the Water Act 1974, Air Act 1981 and EP Act 1986.

Essential points: the "not contrary to municipal law" qualification · the three authorities · the independent domestic route. Common mistake: stating that treaties are automatically part of Indian law — the holding concerns customary international law, and is qualified.

4. What is Sustainable Development according to this judgment, and what are its salient principles?

Model answer. The Court held that the traditional notion that development and ecology are opposed to each other is no longer acceptable; Sustainable Development is the answer — a balancing concept between ecology and development that has been accepted as part of customary international law. It traced the idea from the Stockholm Declaration, 1972, through the Brundtland Report, "Our Common Future" (1987) — development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs — and "Caring for the Earth" (1991), to the Rio Earth Summit of June 1992. From the Brundtland Report and other international documents it culled eight salient principles: inter-generational equity; use and conservation of natural resources; environmental protection; the precautionary principle; the polluter pays principle; the obligation to assist and cooperate; eradication of poverty; and financial assistance to developing countries. Of these, the Court held the precautionary and polluter pays principles to be essential features of Sustainable Development, and applied only those two.

Essential points: balancing concept · the four documents in order · the eight-principle list · only two applied. Common mistake: claiming the Court laid down or applied inter-generational equity — it only listed it.

5. Summarise the directions issued by the Supreme Court.

Model answer. Eleven directions were issued: constitution of an authority under s. 3(3) of the EP Act, 1986 headed by a retired High Court judge, armed with s. 5 powers, by 30 September 1996; assessment by that authority, with NEERI's help, of ecological loss and of individual victims; computation of compensation under two heads — reversing the ecology and payment to individuals — with recovery through the Collector as arrears of land revenue and closure for non-payment; retrospective liability for past pollution notwithstanding subsequent installation of treatment plants; a pollution fine of Rs. 10,000 on each tannery, payable by 31 October 1996 into an Environment Protection Fund; framing of schemes for reversing the damaged ecology in consultation with NEERI and the Boards; installation of a CETP or individual treatment plant by 30 November 1996 with Board consent by 15 December 1996, failing which the units were to be closed; strict enforcement of the restriction on siting highly polluting industries within 1 km of specified water sources, with relocation where necessary; and binding effect for the TDS and other standards approved by NEERI. The Court further requested the Chief Justice of the Madras High Court to constitute a Green Bench, and awarded Rs. 50,000 as costs to Mr. M.C. Mehta.

Essential points: the s. 3(3) authority · two heads · land-revenue recovery · Rs. 10,000 fine and the Fund · CETP deadline and closure · Green Bench. Common mistake: saying the Court ordered the tanneries to be closed. It ordered closure only on default.

Part 19

Long-Answer / Analytical Questions

Q1 — "The Precautionary Principle shifts the burden of proof and thereby transforms environmental adjudication." Discuss with reference to Vellore.

What the examiner is testing: whether you can distinguish the rhetorical part of the principle from its operative part, and connect it to the law of evidence and to judicial review of executive/technical decisions.

Ideal structure: (1) the three limbs; (2) why limb 3 is the operative one; (3) the consequence — the industrialist, not the villager, must produce the science; (4) the institutional consequence — courts need expert assistance (NEERI here); (5) the limits — a principle of precaution is not a principle of prohibition; (6) later refinement.

Key authorities: Vellore; Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action; and, as later development, A.P. Pollution Control Board v. M.V. Nayudu and Narmada Bachao Andolan. Key provisions: Arts. 21, 47, 48-A, 51-A(g); EP Act ss. 3, 5.

Must appear: the exact third limb; that this is a holding of Indian law, not merely an international norm. Extra marks: noting that the reversal makes uncertainty operate against the polluter, and that s. 20 of the NGT Act now makes the principle statutory. Trap: asserting that the principle requires closure of any polluting activity.

Q2 — Examine the relationship between international law and municipal law as laid down in Vellore.

Testing: the doctrine of incorporation, its qualification, and your ability to see that the Court gave an alternative domestic basis.

Structure: (1) the two principles as customary international law; (2) the incorporation rule and the "not contrary to municipal law" limit; (3) the authorities — Gramophone, Jolly George Varghese, ADM Jabalpur; (4) the "even otherwise" domestic route through the Constitution and the three statutes; (5) why the belt-and-braces reasoning matters — the holding survives even if the international-law route is questioned; (6) a note on Article 51(c) and Article 253 as the constitutional setting.

Must appear: the qualification; the alternative route. Extra marks: distinguishing customary international law (incorporated) from treaties (which ordinarily require legislation). Trap: stating flatly that international law is part of Indian law without the qualification.

Q3 — "In Vellore the Supreme Court did not merely declare the law; it built an administrative machine." Critically evaluate the remedial approach.

Testing: your grasp of the Article 32 remedial power, continuing mandamus, and the separation-of-powers objection.

Structure: (1) the five years of interim orders; (2) the eleven directions as an integrated scheme — assessment, quantification, recovery, sanction, restoration, prevention; (3) the use of an existing statutory power (s. 3(3)) rather than a purely judicial creation — the Court's answer to the separation-of-powers objection; (4) the exit to a Green Bench; (5) criticism — judicial fixing of a flat Rs. 10,000 fine without individualised assessment, deadlines set by a court over an industry, capacity of the executive to comply; (6) the later constitutional-tribunal answer in the NGT.

Must appear: that the machinery was hung on a statutory peg. Extra marks: the observation that recovery as arrears of land revenue avoided the need for civil suits. Trap: attributing the directions to Article 142.

Q4 — Trace the development of the Polluter Pays Principle in Indian law, with Vellore at its centre.

Structure: (1) common-law nuisance and Rylands v. Fletcher; (2) absolute liability in the Oleum Gas Leak case as the Indian departure from strict liability not cited in Vellore; (3) Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action (Bichhri) — polluter pays as absolute liability including restoration; (4) Vellore — the principle declared part of Indian law and given machinery; (5) statutory adoption in s. 20 of the NGT Act, 2010.

Must appear: the Bichhri formulation, since that is what Vellore adopted. Trap: presenting the Oleum Gas Leak case as an authority relied on in Vellore; it is background, not a citation in this judgment.

Q5 — "Sustainable Development reconciles the irreconcilable." Test this against the facts of Vellore.

Structure: (1) the factual conflict — 80% of India's finished-leather exports against 35,000 hectares and 350 of 467 wells; (2) the Court's refusal of both extremes; (3) how the balance was actually struck — continue, but treat, pay, restore, and close only on default; (4) whether the balance worked — deadlines of two to three months for an industry that had resisted for a decade; (5) the honest critique: a Rs. 10,000 flat fine is symbolic beside the assessed ecological loss, and the real work fell to the compensation authority; (6) conclusion — sustainable development as a method of allocating burdens, not a formula for avoiding them.

Extra marks: using the Court's own line that remediation is part of sustainable development to show the concept is remedial as well as preventive.

The story — one pass through the whole case

The Palar story

A river dies quietly. Nine hundred tanneries on the Palar turn a chrome-blue stream into 35,000 dead hectares and 350 poisoned wells out of 467. A citizens' forum walks straight into the Supreme Court — no High Court, no appeal, just Article 32 in 1991 — and stays there for five years while the Court sorts industries into lists, sets deadlines, closes some, spares others, and sends NEERI to see whether shared treatment plants can work.

Then the choice. Eighty per cent of India's finished-leather exports on one side; a river on the other. Kuldip Singh, J. refuses the choice: development and ecology are not enemies — Stockholm '72, Brundtland '87, Caring for the Earth '91, Rio '92 — and out of eight fine-sounding principles he takes the two that a court can actually use. Precaution: act before you are certain, and you, the tanner, must prove your process is harmless. Polluter pays: you owe the villagers and you owe the river.

Where does that law come from? Twice over — from customary international law, which slips into Indian law whenever it does not clash with it; and, "even otherwise", from Articles 21, 47, 48-A and 51-A(g) with the Water, Air and Environment Acts. Belt and braces.

And the remedy? Not a sermon. A retired High Court judge in a chair created by s. 3(3), adding up two columns — the ecology's bill and the villagers' bill — handing the total to the Collector, who collects it like land revenue and shuts your gate if you refuse. Rs. 10,000 from every tannery into a fund. Plants in by November or lights out. And then the Supreme Court walks away, leaving a Green Bench in Madras to watch the river, and Rs. 50,000 for the lawyer who brought the case.

Chain to recite in ten seconds: Palar → Article 32 → sustainable development → precaution (onus) + polluter pays (restoration) → customary international law + Arts. 21/47/48-A/51-A(g) → s. 3(3) authority → land revenue → Rs. 10,000 → Green Bench.

Statement to the Collector/District Magistrate; recovered as arrears of land revenue; closure for refusal to pay.

PROCEDURE 03

Who supervises after the judgment?

A Green Bench of the Madras High Court, constituted by its Chief Justice.

HOLDING 01

Was the tanning industry ordered to close?

No. Closure only for units failing to install a CETP/ETP, to obtain consent, or to pay compensation.

HOLDING 02

Is liability for past pollution wiped out by later installing a treatment plant?

No — liability is retrospective in effect for pollution already caused.

HOLDING 03

Two heads of compensation?

(i) Reversing the damaged ecology; (ii) payment to individual victims.

NUMBERS 01

Fine, fund, and costs?

Rs. 10,000 per tannery into the Environment Protection Fund; Rs. 50,000 costs to Mr. M.C. Mehta from the State of Tamil Nadu.

NUMBERS 02

Bench, author, date?

Three judges — Kuldip Singh (author), Faizan Uddin, K. Venkataswami, JJ.; 28 August 1996.

NUMBERS 03

Scale of damage?

35,000 hectares unfit for cultivation; 350 of 467 wells polluted; 900+ tanneries in five districts.

DISTINCTION 01

Vellore or Kamal Nath — which is the public trust doctrine?

M.C. Mehta v. Kamal Nath, (1997) 1 SCC 388. Vellore says nothing about public trust.

DISTINCTION 02

Which later case limits the Precautionary Principle?

Narmada Bachao Andolan v. UOI, (2000) 10 SCC 664 — limited application where effects are known and can be planned for.

DISTINCTION 03

Which river, which State — and what is the other tannery case?

Palar, Tamil Nadu. The other is M.C. Mehta (Ganga Pollution — Kanpur tanneries), (1987) 4 SCC 463.

TRAP 01

Is inter-generational equity the ratio of Vellore?

No — it is one of eight listed salient principles; only precaution and polluter pays were applied.

TRAP 02

Was Article 142 invoked?

Not established from the judgment. The jurisdiction exercised is Article 32.

AFTER

Where do these principles live in statute today?

Section 20, National Green Tribunal Act, 2010 — sustainable development, precautionary principle, polluter pays principle. later development

AOR Last-Night Revision Card

Read only this, in the corridor

Case
Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum v. Union of India, (1996) 5 SCC 647 · 28.08.1996 · Kuldip Singh, Faizan Uddin, K. Venkataswami, JJ. · authored by Kuldip Singh, J. · W.P.(C) 914/1991 under Art. 32.
Facts
900+ tanneries in five Tamil Nadu districts discharged untreated effluent into the Palar river and groundwater; 35,000 hectares unfit for cultivation, 350 of 467 wells polluted, though the State supplies ~80% of India's finished-leather exports.
Issues
Is there an Art. 21 violation? Must industry close, or can it continue? Are precaution and polluter pays part of Indian law? What machinery fixes and recovers liability?
Provisions
Arts. 21, 47, 48-A, 51-A(g); Water Act 1974; Air Act 1981; EP Act 1986, ss. 3(3) and 5.
Ratio
Sustainable development balances ecology and development; the Precautionary Principle (anticipate–prevent–attack; uncertainty is no excuse where damage is serious and irreversible; onus on the industrialist to prove his act is environmentally benign) and the Polluter Pays Principle (absolute liability = compensation to victims + cost of restoring the ecology; remediation is part of sustainable development) are essential features of sustainable development and part of the environmental law of India — as customary international law not contrary to municipal law, and, "even otherwise", under the Constitution and the three Acts.
Key precedent
Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action (Bichhri) for polluter pays; Gramophone Co., Jolly George Varghese, ADM Jabalpur for incorporation of customary international law.
Procedure
Art. 32 original jurisdiction; five years of continuing mandamus; NEERI as expert; exit by handing supervision to a Green Bench of the Madras High Court. Not Art. 142.
Holding
Petition allowed; eleven directions — s. 3(3) authority headed by a retired High Court judge (by 30.09.1996); compensation under two heads recovered by the Collector as arrears of land revenue; Rs. 10,000 fine per tannery into the Environment Protection Fund; CETP/ETP by 30.11.1996 and consent by 15.12.1996 or closure; liability for past pollution retrospective; Rs. 50,000 costs to Mr. M.C. Mehta.
Exception
Industry is not shut down for polluting as such — closure follows only on default in treatment, consent or payment.
Trap
Three judges, not a Constitution Bench. Only two of the eight listed principles were applied — inter-generational equity was merely listed. Palar, not Ganga; Madras, not Calcutta.
Takeaway
The polluter must prove himself harmless, and if he is not, he pays the victim and repairs the river.
