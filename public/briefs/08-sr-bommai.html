S.R. Bommai v. Union of India

President's Rule is not beyond the courts. A government's majority is tested on the floor of the House, not in the Governor's drawing room — and a State that acts against secularism can be dismissed for it.

(1994) 3 SCC 1AIR 1994 SC 1918[1994] 2 SCR 64411 March 19949 Judges · six opinions

Read this first — six States, two outcomes

The case decided six Proclamations under Article 356. Three were held unconstitutional — Karnataka (1989), Nagaland (1988), Meghalaya (1991). Three were upheld — Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh (15 December 1992, after the Babri Masjid demolition). The first group gives you the law on judicial review and the floor test; the second gives you secularism as a ground for Article 356.

There is no single majority opinion. Six opinions were delivered. The law is taken from the points on which Sawant J. (with Kuldip Singh J.) and Jeevan Reddy J. (with Agrawal J.), supported by Pandian J., agree — five of nine. Strong

On sources. No judgment file was uploaded. I worked from the Supreme Court's head-notes volume and the settled reported position. §30 lists what I could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Trigger. In April 1989 the Governor of Karnataka reported that Chief Minister S.R. Bommai had lost his majority, on the strength of letters from legislators, and refused him a floor test. President's Rule followed.
  2. Batch. Similar dismissals in Nagaland and Meghalaya, and the 1992 dismissals of three BJP governments, were heard together.
  3. Issue. Is a Proclamation under Article 356 justiciable, and on what grounds? Can the Assembly be dissolved at once?
  4. Rule. The Proclamation is subject to judicial review. The President's satisfaction must rest on relevant material. Article 74(2) shields the advice, not the material.
  5. Floor test. The majority of a Ministry is to be tested on the floor of the House.
  6. Dissolution. The Assembly may only be suspended until both Houses of Parliament approve the Proclamation; a court may restore the dismissed government.
  7. Secularism is a basic feature; a State government acting against it is not being carried on in accordance with the Constitution.

03One-Line Ratio

The sentence to reproduce

The ratio in one line

A Proclamation under Article 356 is judicially reviewable to examine whether it was issued on relevant material and not mala fide; the Legislative Assembly cannot be dissolved before Parliament approves the Proclamation; the strength of a Ministry is to be tested on the floor of the House; and secularism is a basic feature whose violation by a State government can justify action under Article 356. Certain

05Procedural History

How it reached the Court

  1. Karnataka. Bommai's writ petition was dismissed by a Full Bench of the Karnataka High Court; he appealed. Strong
  2. Nagaland. The Gauhati High Court was divided; the matter came up to the Supreme Court. Strong
  3. Meghalaya. Challenged directly/through transfer. Strong
  4. Madhya Pradesh. The High Court struck down the Proclamation; the Union appealed. Rajasthan and Himachal petitions were transferred to the Supreme Court. Strong
  5. All matters were referred to nine Judges because State of Rajasthan v. Union of India (1977) was a seven-judge decision.

07Relevant Law

The provisions in play

ProvisionContentHow read

08Ratio Decidendi

The propositions

1 — Judicial review

Rule. The Proclamation is not immune. The court asks: was there any material; was it relevant to the action; was the action mala fide or based on wholly irrelevant or extraneous grounds. Limit. The court does not weigh the adequacy or sufficiency of the material or substitute its opinion. Burden. Once a prima facie case is made out, the Union must show that relevant material existed. Certain

2 — Article 74(2)

The bar is on inquiry into what advice was given. The material on which the President formed his satisfaction is not advice and can be called for; privilege under the Evidence Act may be claimed separately. Certain

3 — Floor test

Whether a Ministry enjoys the confidence of the House is a matter for the House. The Governor's private assessment, based on letters or head-counts, is no substitute. Where the Governor does not resort to it, the reasons must be exceptional and recorded. Certain

4 — No dissolution before Parliamentary approval

On issuing the Proclamation the President may suspend the Assembly. Dissolution — an irreversible step — can be ordered only after both Houses approve. If the Proclamation is not approved, it lapses, and the dismissed government and suspended Assembly revive. Certain

5 — Relief

If the Proclamation is held invalid, the court may restore the status quo ante — revive the Assembly and reinstate the Ministry — notwithstanding approval by Parliament. It may also stay fresh elections pending the challenge. Relief remains discretionary. Certain

6 — Secularism and federalism

Secularism is part of the basic structure: the State treats all religions equally and does not mix religion with State power. A State government that pursues unsecular policies acts contrary to the Constitution, and that is relevant material for Article 356. Federalism and democracy are likewise basic features; States are not appendages of the Centre. Certain

13Dissent & Concurring Opinions

Separate opinions

  • Sawant J. (with Kuldip Singh J.). The fullest statement of the propositions in §8; Karnataka tainted by haste and mala fides; Meghalaya's material prima facie irrational; Nagaland bad for denying a floor test. Certain
  • Jeevan Reddy J. (with Agrawal J.). Agrees on review, Article 74(2), approval and relief; treats the power to dissolve as existing under Article 356(1)(a) but exercisable only after approval; lists his conclusions in numbered form. Certain
  • Pandian J. Agrees with Jeevan Reddy J. and with Sawant J.'s conclusions. Strong
  • Ahmadi J. Narrow review; agrees on secularism; would not interfere with the earlier Proclamations. Strong
  • Verma J. (with Dayal J.). Only cases permitting application of judicially manageable standards are justiciable; a floor test is the norm. Strong
  • K. Ramaswamy J. Narrow review; extended treatment of secularism; religion cannot be mixed with politics. Strong

14Final Holding

The operative result

  • Proclamations in Karnataka (21.4.1989), Nagaland (7.8.1988) and Meghalaya (11.10.1991) — unconstitutional. Certain
  • Proclamations in Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh (15.12.1992) — not unconstitutional. The Madhya Pradesh High Court's contrary decision was reversed. Certain

15Directions & Relief

What was ordered

  • No restoration of the Ministries or Assemblies in Karnataka, Nagaland or Meghalaya, because fresh elections had since been held and new governments formed. Certain
  • Acts done during the period of the invalid Proclamations were saved. Certain

Not established from the judgment: any award of costs or damages.

16AOR-Specific Importance

Why an AOR candidate must own this case

  • Interim relief is everything. Bommai shows why: by the time the Karnataka appeal was decided the remedy was academic. A petition against a Proclamation must seek an immediate stay of dissolution and of fresh elections.
  • Production of material. Plead that Article 74(2) is no bar and call for the record.
  • Floor test. The standard prayer in every government-formation dispute traces to this case.
  • Secularism as a justiciable standard. Authority for pleading secularism as a basic feature.
  • Plural opinions. Practise stating which propositions command five votes.
  • Links. Rameshwar Prasad (22), Kihoto Hollohan (6), Keisham Meghachandra (59).

172-Mark Questions

Answer in two or three lines

Q1Is a Proclamation under Article 356 open to judicial review?

Yes. The court examines whether it was based on relevant material and was not mala fide; it does not examine the sufficiency of the material.

Q2What does Article 74(2) protect?

Only the advice tendered by Ministers to the President — not the material on which the advice was based.

Q3What is the floor test?

The rule that a Ministry's majority must be tested on the floor of the Legislative Assembly, not by the Governor's personal assessment.

Q4When can the Assembly be dissolved under Article 356?

Only after both Houses of Parliament approve the Proclamation; until then it may only be suspended.

Q5Which Proclamations were upheld?

Madhya Pradesh, Rajasthan and Himachal Pradesh, of 15 December 1992.

Q6Which basic features did the Court affirm?

Secularism, federalism and democracy.

185-Mark Questions

Answer in about 150 words

Q1State the scope of judicial review of a Proclamation under Article 356.

The power is conditioned on the President's satisfaction that the State government cannot be carried on in accordance with the Constitution. That satisfaction, though subjective, must be formed on objective material. The court may examine whether any material existed, whether it was relevant, and whether the power was used mala fide or for extraneous purposes. It will not examine the adequacy of the material or the correctness of the conclusion. Article 74(2) does not prevent production of the material. If the Proclamation fails the test it can be struck down even after Parliament has approved it, and the court may restore the Ministry and the Assembly.

Q2Why were the Karnataka and Nagaland Proclamations struck down?

In both, the Governor decided for himself that the Ministry (Karnataka) or a claimant (Nagaland) lacked a majority and denied a test on the floor of the House. In Karnataka several legislators had retracted their letters and the Chief Minister had offered to face the House within days. The Governor's report was hasty, and the President's satisfaction based on it rested on no relevant material.

Q3How did secularism justify the 1992 dismissals?

Secularism is a basic feature. The governments of the three States belonged to a party whose leaders and manifesto were found to have supported the events at Ayodhya; some Ministers were associated with banned organisations. A government pursuing policies contrary to secularism is not acting in accordance with the Constitution; that was relevant material, and the court could not go into its sufficiency.

Q4Distinguish State of Rajasthan (1977) from Bommai.
  • Rajasthan: seven Judges; review theoretically available but confined to mala fides; Article 74(2) treated as shielding the reasons; immediate dissolution upheld.
  • Bommai: nine Judges; review of the existence and relevance of material; Article 74(2) does not shield material; dissolution only after Parliamentary approval; restoration possible.

19Long-Answer Questions

Structure for a 15–20 mark answer

Q1"Bommai turned Article 356 from a political weapon into a constitutional power." Discuss.

Introduction. Text and history of Art. 356; frequency of use; Sarkaria Commission. Before. State of Rajasthan; 38th and 44th Amendments. Facts. Six Proclamations in two groups. Law laid down. Review; Art. 74(2); floor test; suspension not dissolution; restoration; interim relief. Secularism and federalism. Basic features; the 1992 cases. Division on the Bench. Five and four; where they agree. After. Rameshwar Prasad; Uttarakhand; Nabam Rebia. Critique. Relief came too late in Bommai itself; "unsecular" as a standard. Conclusion.

Q2Discuss secularism as a basic feature with reference to S.R. Bommai.

Meaning — equal treatment, no State religion, religion kept out of State power. Sources — Preamble (42nd Amendment), Arts. 14–16, 25–30, 51A. Kesavananda; Bommai — positive concept; political parties and religion; s. 123 RPA. Application — the three States. Later — Ismail Faruqui (1994); Abhiram Singh (2017). Conclusion.

22Top 10 Exam Traps

Ten ways to lose marks

  1. Saying the Court restored the Bommai government.
  2. Saying all six Proclamations were struck down.
  3. Writing that the court examines sufficiency of material.
  4. Treating Article 74(2) as a complete bar.
  5. Calling the judgment unanimous or citing "the majority judgment" without naming the opinions.
  6. Saying Parliamentary approval cures an invalid Proclamation.
  7. Omitting secularism — half the case.
  8. Saying the Assembly can never be dissolved under Article 356.
  9. Confusing the Governor's report with the President's satisfaction.
  10. Giving the Bench as seven.

Facts. Six Proclamations under Art. 356. Karnataka (1989): Governor acted on MLAs' letters, refused Bommai a floor test. Nagaland (1988), Meghalaya (1991): similar. M.P., Rajasthan, H.P. (15.12.1992): BJP governments dismissed after Ayodhya.
