AOR Paper IVLeading Case 11 of 86Master Brief

D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal

A letter about deaths in police lock-ups became eleven rules that every arresting officer in India must follow — and the authority for awarding compensation against the State when they are broken.

(1997) 1 SCC 416AIR 1997 SC 610[1996] Supp. 10 SCR 28418 December 19962 Judges · unanimous

Read this first — the requirements are now largely statute

The eleven requirements were framed to operate until legislation was made. Much of it has been: the Code of Criminal Procedure (Amendment) Act, 2008 inserted sections 41A–41D, 50A and strengthened section 54, and the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 carries those provisions forward (sections 35–38 and 48 onward). Strong In a 2027 answer, D.K. Basu is the constitutional source and the authority on public-law compensation; the Sanhita is the day-to-day procedure.

Bench strength. This is a two-judge decision. It is on the list because of its reach, not its Bench.

On sources. No judgment file was uploaded. I worked from the Supreme Court's head-notes volume and the settled reported position. §30 lists what I could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Trigger. On 26 August 1986 D.K. Basu, Executive Chairman of Legal Aid Services, West Bengal, wrote to the Chief Justice of India about newspaper reports of deaths in police custody.
  2. Petition. The letter was treated as a writ petition under Article 32. A similar letter about a custodial death in Aligarh was tagged. Notice went to all States.
  3. Issue. How is custodial violence to be prevented, and what remedy lies when it occurs?
  4. Rule. Custodial torture and death strike at Article 21. The right to life does not end at the police station door.
  5. Requirements. Eleven procedural requirements for every arrest and detention, in addition to existing safeguards.
  6. Sanction. Breach attracts departmental action and contempt of court.
  7. Compensation. Monetary compensation is an appropriate public-law remedy under Articles 32 and 226 for established violation of Article 21; sovereign immunity is no defence.

03One-Line Ratio

The sentence to reproduce

The ratio in one line

Custodial violence violates Article 21; to prevent it, the police and all other arresting agencies must observe the prescribed requirements on arrest and detention, failure of which is punishable departmentally and as contempt; and for established infringement of Article 21 the courts under Articles 32 and 226 will award compensation against the State on the footing of strict liability, to which sovereign immunity is not a defence. Certain

04Facts

What actually happened

  • 26 August 1986. D.K. Basu's letter to the Chief Justice enclosed news reports from July 1986 about deaths in police lock-ups and custody and asked the Court to develop "custody jurisprudence" and fix compensation and accountability. Strong
  • The letter was treated as a writ petition; notice issued to the State of West Bengal, which replied that the police were not hushing up any matter. Strong
  • 29 July 1987. Ashok Kumar Johri wrote about the death of Mahesh Bihari of Pilkhana, Aligarh in police custody. That too was registered and tagged. Strong
  • 14 August 1987. Considering the frequency of such complaints from across the country, the Court issued notice to all State Governments and to the Law Commission for suggestions. Strong
  • Several States filed affidavits. Dr. A.M. Singhvi assisted the Court as amicus curiae. Strong

05Procedural History

How it reached the Court

  1. No lower-court history — the matter began and ended in the Supreme Court under Article 32.
  2. Epistolary jurisdiction: letter → writ petition (crl.).
  3. Converted from a State-specific complaint into an all-India inquiry by the order of 14 August 1987. Strong
  4. Heard with the assistance of an amicus; judgment 18 December 1996.
  5. The petition was kept alive after judgment for monitoring; further orders followed for nearly two decades. Strong

08Ratio Decidendi

Two limbs

Limb A — the eleven requirements

To be followed in all cases of arrest or detention until legal provisions are made: Certain

  1. Identification. Arresting and interrogating officers to wear accurate, visible and clear name tags with designations; particulars of interrogating officers recorded in a register.
  2. Memo of arrest. Prepared at the time of arrest; attested by at least one witness (a family member or a respectable person of the locality); countersigned by the arrestee; with time and date.
  3. Right to have someone informed. A friend, relative or other person interested in the arrestee's welfare to be told of the arrest and place of detention as soon as practicable — unless the attesting witness is such a person.
  4. Out-of-district intimation. If that person lives outside the district or town, time, place of arrest and venue of custody to be notified through the Legal Aid Organisation in the district and the police station concerned, telegraphically, within 8 to 12 hours.
  5. Told of the right. The arrestee must be made aware of the right to have someone informed.
  6. Diary entry. Entry at the place of detention recording the arrest, the name of the person informed, and the officers in whose custody the arrestee is.
  7. Inspection memo. On request, examination at the time of arrest; major and minor injuries recorded; memo signed by arrestee and officer; copy to the arrestee.
  8. Medical examination every 48 hours during custody, by a doctor on an approved panel.
  9. Copies to the Magistrate. All documents, including the memo of arrest, sent to the Illaqa Magistrate.
  10. Lawyer. The arrestee may be permitted to meet a lawyer during interrogation, though not throughout it.
  11. Police control room at every district and State headquarters; information on the arrest and place of custody communicated within 12 hours and displayed on a conspicuous notice board.

Reach. The requirements bind not only the police but other agencies with powers of detention and interrogation — the head-note records that they extend to other governmental agencies. Certain They are in addition to, not in derogation of, existing constitutional and statutory safeguards and earlier directions of the Court. Certain

Enforcement. Failure renders the official liable to departmental action and to be punished for contempt of court; contempt proceedings may be instituted in the High Court having territorial jurisdiction. Certain

Limb B — compensation

Rule. For established violation of Article 21, the Supreme Court and High Courts may award monetary compensation as a public-law remedy. Basis. Strict liability of the State for the acts of its servants; the defence of sovereign immunity is not available against a claim for breach of fundamental rights. Nature. It is in addition to — not in substitution for — a civil suit for damages and criminal prosecution; the amount may be adjusted against damages later awarded in a civil suit. Quantum. No strait-jacket formula; depends on the facts. Certain

09Obiter & Observations

What is not binding

  • The Court's endorsement of the Law Commission's 113th Report, recommending a new section 114B in the Evidence Act (presumption that injuries in custody were caused by the police), is a recommendation to Parliament. Strong
  • Observations on balancing the needs of interrogation against individual rights — the State may interrogate with scientific methods, but cannot torture, even in terrorism cases. Certain
  • Remarks on training and attitudinal change in the police force.

10Judicial Reasoning

How the Court reasoned

  1. Torture is an assault on dignity. It is not defined in the Constitution or penal law, yet it is the gravest form of abuse of State power.
  2. Fundamental rights survive arrest. Convicts, undertrials and detainees retain Article 21; restrictions must be those permitted by law.
  3. Existing law is not working. Custodial crimes are committed within the four walls of a police station; direct evidence is rarely available; policemen do not testify against colleagues (State of M.P. v. Shyamsunder Trivedi, 1995). Strong
  4. Transparency and accountability are the best safeguards — hence documents, witnesses, intimation and medical checks that leave a record.
  5. Balance. Crime must be investigated, but the State's actions must be "right, just and fair"; third-degree methods are impermissible however grave the offence. Certain
  6. Remedy. Ubi jus ibi remedium. A declaration that rights were violated is not enough; the public-law court must mulct the wrongdoer and compensate the victim. Article 9(5) of the ICCPR supports an enforceable right to compensation, India's reservation notwithstanding. Strong

12Precedent Chain

Where the case sits in the line

Later developments — not part of this judgment

  • Follow-up orders in the same petition continued for years; in 2015 the Court issued further directions on State Human Rights Commissions and CCTV in prisons and police stations. Strong
  • Paramvir Singh Saini v. Baljit Singh (2020) — CCTV with audio in every police station and in offices of central agencies. Strong
  • Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 re-enacts the arrest provisions, including the memo of arrest, intimation to a nominated person, control rooms and medical examination. Strong

14Final Holding

The operative result

  • Custodial violence, including torture and death in lock-up, violates Article 21. Certain
  • The eleven requirements are to be followed in all cases of arrest or detention till legal provisions are made. Certain
  • Non-compliance — departmental action and contempt. Certain
  • Compensation is available in public law for established violation of Article 21; sovereign immunity does not apply. Certain

15Directions & Relief

What was ordered

  • The requirements to be forwarded to the Director General of Police and Home Secretary of every State and Union Territory, and circulated to every police station. Certain
  • To be publicised — including by broadcast on All India Radio and Doordarshan and by pamphlets in local languages. Strong
  • To be displayed at police stations at a conspicuous place. Strong
  • The requirements to apply, with suitable changes, to other agencies such as the DRI, Enforcement Directorate, CBI, CRPF, BSF and the like. Strong

Not established from the judgment: any specific sum of compensation awarded in the two individual custodial-death complaints.

16AOR-Specific Importance

Why an AOR candidate must own this case

  • Drafting a habeas corpus or compensation petition. Plead each requirement breached — no arrest memo, no intimation, no medical examination — as a distinct violation of Article 21.
  • Contempt route. Non-compliance is contempt of the Supreme Court's order, to be moved in the High Court; a useful, quicker remedy than a writ for damages.
  • Compensation. The authority, with Nilabati Behera, that a writ court awards money; plead strict liability and that sovereign immunity is irrelevant.
  • Bail and remand arguments. Illegal arrest for want of the memo or intimation supports release.
  • Epistolary jurisdiction. A model of a letter petition growing into continuing mandamus.
  • Links. Maneka Gandhi (2), Lalita Kumari (31), Selvi (26), Sushila Aggarwal (60).

172-Mark Questions

Answer in two or three lines

Q1How did the case originate?

From a letter dated 26 August 1986 by D.K. Basu, Executive Chairman, Legal Aid Services, West Bengal, to the Chief Justice of India about custodial deaths, treated as a writ petition under Article 32.

Q2State the Bench.

Two Judges — Kuldip Singh and Dr. A.S. Anand JJ.; judgment by Dr. Anand J.

Q3What must a memo of arrest contain?

Time and date of arrest; attestation by at least one witness (family member or respectable local person); countersignature of the arrestee.

Q4How often must an arrestee in custody be medically examined?

Every 48 hours, by a doctor on the approved panel.

Q5What is the consequence of breaching the requirements?

Departmental action and punishment for contempt of court, in the High Court with territorial jurisdiction.

Q6Is sovereign immunity a defence to a claim for compensation for custodial death?

No. It has no application to a public-law claim for violation of fundamental rights.

185-Mark Questions

Answer in about 150 words

Q1Explain the basis on which compensation is awarded in public law.

A violation of Article 21 by State agents is a constitutional wrong for which the State is strictly liable. The remedy under Articles 32 and 226 is not confined to declarations; the court can award monetary compensation to redress the wrong and to penalise the wrongdoer. This is distinct from a private-law action in tort, where sovereign immunity (Kasturilal) might be pleaded; that doctrine is alien to the enforcement of fundamental rights. The award does not bar a civil suit or prosecution, and any sum paid can be set off against damages later decreed. Quantum depends on the facts. The principle derives from Rudul Sah and Nilabati Behera and is restated in D.K. Basu.

Q2Why did the Court think new requirements were necessary despite the CrPC?

Because custodial violence continued despite constitutional and statutory safeguards. The offence occurs in secrecy; witnesses are policemen; convictions are rare. The Court concluded that transparency of action and accountability were the effective checks, and so required a paper trail — arrest memo, diary entries, inspection memo, intimation to relatives and the Magistrate, periodic medical examination — and external eyes: a witness, a lawyer, a control-room notice board.

Q3To what extent is the arrestee entitled to a lawyer during interrogation?

The arrestee may be permitted to meet his lawyer during interrogation, though not throughout the interrogation. The right is to consultation, not continuous presence.

Q4What is the present statutory position?

The 2008 CrPC amendments, in force from 2010, enacted the memo of arrest, name tags, intimation, control rooms and the right to meet an advocate (sections 41B–41D, 50A). The BNSS 2023 continues them. D.K. Basu remains the constitutional yardstick and the basis for contempt and compensation. Strong

19Long-Answer Questions

Structure for a 15–20 mark answer

Q1"D.K. Basu constitutionalised the law of arrest." Discuss.

Introduction. Letter petition; Bench; date. The problem. Custodial violence; evidentiary difficulty. Constitutional basis. Arts. 21, 22, 20(3); dignity. The requirements. Group them — identification, documentation, intimation, medical, legal access, oversight. Enforcement. Contempt; departmental action; all agencies. Compensation. Public law; strict liability; sovereign immunity. Precedents. Joginder Kumar; Nilabati Behera; Rudul Sah. After. 2008 amendments; Arnesh Kumar; Paramvir Singh Saini; BNSS. Critique. Implementation gaps; no anti-torture statute. Conclusion.

Q2Discuss the evolution of compensation as a public-law remedy for violation of Article 21.

Khatri (1981) — question posed. Rudul Sah (1983) — first award. Sebastian Hongray, Bhim Singh (1984–85). Saheli (1990). Nilabati Behera (1993) — doctrinal basis; sovereign immunity. D.K. Basu (1996) — consolidated. Later — medical negligence and State liability cases. Conclusion.

22Top 10 Exam Traps

Ten ways to lose marks

  1. Calling it a Constitution Bench decision.
  2. Writing "guidelines" only — the Court called them requirements and backed them with contempt.
  3. Saying a lawyer may be present throughout interrogation.
  4. Mixing the 8–12 hour and 12 hour limits.
  5. Forgetting the witness to the memo of arrest.
  6. Omitting the compensation limb.
  7. Saying compensation replaces a civil suit.
  8. Citing Kasturilal as supporting the claimant.
  9. Confining the requirements to the police — they extend to other agencies.
  10. Ignoring the statutory codification when asked for the current law.
