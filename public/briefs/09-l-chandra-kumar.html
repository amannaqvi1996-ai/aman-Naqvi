L. Chandra Kumar v. Union of India

Tribunals may decide first, but they cannot decide last. The power of the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 and of the Supreme Court under Article 32 to review legislation and tribunal orders is part of the basic structure and cannot be taken away.

(1995) 1 SCC 400[1994] Supp. 6 SCR 261(1997) 3 SCC 261AIR 1997 SC 1125Reference 2 December 1994 · Answer 18 March 19973 Judges, then 7 · unanimous

Read this first — the list cites the reference order, not the famous judgment

The Supreme Court's list gives this case as [1994] Supp. 6 SCR 261 / (1995) 1 SCC 400. That is the three-judge reference order of 2 December 1994, and the official head-note for it runs to two pages and records only the questions referred. Certain The decision everyone means by "L. Chandra Kumar" is the seven-judge judgment of 18 March 1997, (1997) 3 SCC 261, which answered the reference.

What to do in the exam. Cite both. Give the 1994 citation because it is the prescribed one, state what was referred, and then give the 1997 holdings — an examiner asking about this case wants the law on judicial review and tribunals. This brief marks every proposition as [Ref] (1994) or [7J] (1997).

On sources. No judgment file was uploaded. The 1994 material is from the Supreme Court's head-notes volume. The 1997 holdings are from the settled reported position, not from that volume, and are tagged accordingly. §30 lists what I could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Background. The 42nd Amendment added Articles 323A and 323B, allowing tribunals and the exclusion of all courts except the Supreme Court under Article 136. The Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 did exactly that (section 28).
  2. Sampath Kumar (1987) upheld the Act: a tribunal can be an "effective substitute" for the High Court.
  3. Reference (1994). Three Judges, hearing a challenge involving section 5(6) of the Act, doubted Sampath Kumar and referred it to a larger Bench.
  4. Issue. Can the High Courts' jurisdiction under Articles 226/227 and this Court's under Article 32 be excluded? Can tribunals decide constitutional validity?
  5. Ratio (1997). Judicial review under Articles 32, 226 and 227 is part of the basic structure. It cannot be ousted. Tribunals are supplemental, not substitutes.
  6. Holding. Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d) and section 28 are unconstitutional to the extent they exclude that jurisdiction. Tribunal decisions go before a Division Bench of the High Court.
  7. Significance. The foundation of all later tribunal litigation — R. Gandhi, Madras Bar Association, Rojer Mathew.

03One-Line Ratio

The sentence to reproduce

The ratio in one line

The power of judicial review vested in the High Courts under Articles 226 and 227 and in the Supreme Court under Article 32 is part of the basic structure; tribunals created under Articles 323A and 323B can only supplement, not supplant, that power, and their decisions are subject to scrutiny by a Division Bench of the High Court. Strong

04Facts

What actually happened

  • 1976. The 42nd Amendment inserted Part XIVA — Article 323A (administrative tribunals for service matters) and Article 323B (tribunals for other matters such as tax, land reform, industrial disputes). Clause (2)(d) of 323A and clause (3)(d) of 323B authorised laws excluding the jurisdiction of all courts except the Supreme Court under Article 136. Strong
  • 1985. Parliament enacted the Administrative Tribunals Act. Section 28 excluded the jurisdiction of all courts in service matters, save the Supreme Court. Service writ petitions pending in High Courts were transferred to the tribunals. Strong
  • 1987. S.P. Sampath Kumar v. Union of India (five Judges) upheld the Act: judicial review may be vested in an alternative institutional mechanism if it is no less effective than the High Court. Strong
  • After 1987. Doubts grew — over section 5(6), which lets a single Member (even an Administrative Member) hear cases; over tribunals deciding the validity of statutes; and over how tribunals actually functioned (R.K. Jain v. Union of India, 1993). A Full Bench of the Andhra Pradesh High Court (Sakinala Harinath, 1993) held Article 323A(2)(d) unconstitutional. Strong
  • 2 December 1994. A three-judge Bench, hearing a matter concerning section 5(6), referred the correctness of Sampath Kumar and the later cases to a larger Bench. Certain

05Procedural History

How it reached the Court

  1. A batch of petitions and appeals from different High Courts and tribunals raised overlapping challenges to the tribunal system. Strong
  2. [Ref] The three-judge Bench (Kuldip Singh, Hansaria, Majmudar JJ.) referred these questions: Certain
  • whether tribunals can be equated with High Courts;
  • the jurisdiction and authority of administrative tribunals to decide the constitutional validity of legislation and rules;
  • whether a tribunal that can decide the validity of a rule under Article 309 can include an Administrative Member;
  • whether the judicial review held available to tribunals in Sampath Kumar violates the basic structure.
  1. Because Sampath Kumar was by five Judges, the matter was placed before seven. Strong
  2. 18 March 1997. Unanimous judgment by Ahmadi C.J. Strong

08Ratio Decidendi

The propositions — all [7J]

1 — Judicial review is a basic feature

Rule. The power of the High Courts and the Supreme Court to test legislative and executive action against the Constitution is integral to the constitutional scheme and part of its basic structure. Reasoning. Judges of the superior courts have constitutional safeguards of independence that tribunal members lack; the framers entrusted interpretation of the Constitution to them. Result. That power cannot be ousted or excluded, even by constitutional amendment. Strong

2 — The exclusion clauses fall

Articles 323A(2)(d) and 323B(3)(d), section 28 of the 1985 Act, and like clauses in other legislation are unconstitutional to the extent they exclude the jurisdiction of the High Courts under Articles 226/227 and of the Supreme Court under Article 32. The Articles and the Act otherwise stand. Strong

3 — Tribunals are supplemental

Tribunals may continue to perform a supplemental role. They are competent to hear matters where the vires of statutory provisions or rules are questioned — except a challenge to the vires of their parent statute, since a creature of a statute cannot declare that statute void. Strong

4 — Courts of first instance

Tribunals function as courts of first instance in the areas of law for which they are constituted. A litigant cannot go directly to the High Court, even when the validity of a statute is questioned (parent statute apart), bypassing the tribunal. Strong

5 — Division Bench scrutiny

All decisions of tribunals are subject to scrutiny before a Division Bench of the High Court within whose territorial jurisdiction the tribunal falls. No appeal lies directly to the Supreme Court under Article 136 from a tribunal's order; the aggrieved party goes to the High Court first, and from the Division Bench to the Supreme Court. Strong

6 — Sampath Kumar disapproved in part

The theory that a tribunal can be a substitute for the High Court is rejected. Sampath Kumar is not good law to that extent. Strong

09Obiter & Observations

What is not binding

The Court made recommendations, not directions, on tribunal administration: Strong

  • tribunals should, as an interim measure, be brought under a single nodal Ministry — the Ministry of Law — to oversee their working, pending a wholly independent supervisory agency;
  • the appointment of Administrative Members need not be stopped; technical experience is valuable when combined with judicial members;
  • selection should not be dominated by the executive. Strong

These were taken further in R. Gandhi (2010), Rojer Mathew (2019) and the Madras Bar Association cases.

10Judicial Reasoning

How the Court reasoned

  1. Start with basic structure. Kesavananda, Indira Gandhi and Minerva Mills treat judicial review as basic.
  2. Why High Courts matter. Articles 226 and 227 give every citizen an accessible constitutional court; superintendence over tribunals is part of that design.
  3. Why tribunals cannot replace them. Tribunal members do not have the tenure, conditions of service or independence of High Court judges; the working of tribunals had drawn criticism (Malimath Committee; R.K. Jain). Strong
  4. But tribunals are needed. Arrears in High Courts are real; to abolish tribunals would be to go back. Hence retention with a supervisory layer.
  5. Balance. Tribunal first — to filter and to give the High Court the benefit of a reasoned decision; Division Bench second — to preserve constitutional review.
  6. Section 5(6). Saved by harmonious reading rather than struck down. Strong

12Precedent Chain

Where the case sits in the line

Later developments — not part of this judgment

  • Direct statutory appeals. Several statutes provide an appeal from a tribunal straight to the Supreme Court (for example from the Armed Forces Tribunal or NCLAT). Rojer Mathew (2019) asked that this be reconsidered. The High Court's power under Article 226 over such tribunals has been reaffirmed, though it is exercised with restraint. Strong
  • Tribunals Reforms Act, 2021 abolished several tribunals and moved their work to the High Courts. Strong
  • Contempt. T. Sudhakar Prasad v. Govt. of A.P. (2001) held that tribunals retain their contempt power after L. Chandra Kumar. Strong

14Final Holding

The operative result

  • Clause 2(d) of Article 323A and clause 3(d) of Article 323B, to the extent they exclude the jurisdiction of the High Courts and the Supreme Court under Articles 226/227 and 32, are unconstitutional. Strong
  • Section 28 of the Administrative Tribunals Act, 1985 and "exclusion of jurisdiction" clauses in all other legislation enacted under Articles 323A and 323B are unconstitutional to the same extent. Strong
  • Tribunals continue as courts of first instance; their decisions are subject to Division Bench scrutiny. Strong
  • Section 5(6) is valid as read down. Strong

15Directions & Relief

What was ordered

  • The individual matters were directed to be listed before a Division Bench for decision on merits in light of the law declared. Strong
  • The direction on Division Bench scrutiny was stated to operate prospectively. Strong

Not established from the judgment: the outcome of L. Chandra Kumar's own service dispute.

16AOR-Specific Importance

Why an AOR candidate must own this case

  • Maintainability — the commonest practice point on the list. An SLP directly against an order of the Central or a State Administrative Tribunal is not entertained; the petitioner must first approach the High Court under Article 226/227. Registry and Bench will both raise it.
  • Forum. Writ against a tribunal order goes before a Division Bench, not a Single Judge.
  • Alternative remedy. A writ petition filed in the High Court without first going to the tribunal is liable to be rejected.
  • Vires challenges. A challenge to the tribunal's own parent Act goes straight to the High Court; all other vires challenges start in the tribunal.
  • Drafting. In a synopsis, state the route taken — tribunal order, High Court order — with dates.
  • Links. Rojer Mathew (55), Madras Bar Association (64), Kihoto Hollohan (6), Minerva Mills (3).

185-Mark Questions

Answer in about 150 words

Q1Why did the Court hold that tribunals cannot be substitutes for High Courts?

Judicial review of legislation and of administrative action is entrusted by the Constitution to the Supreme Court and the High Courts, whose judges enjoy constitutional guarantees of independence. Tribunal members are appointed and serviced differently and lack those guarantees; the experience of tribunals' functioning had been unsatisfactory. Judicial review under Articles 32, 226 and 227 is part of the basic structure, so it cannot be transferred wholesale to a body that is not a constitutional court. Sampath Kumar's premise that a tribunal could be an equally effective alternative was therefore rejected. Tribunals may assist — they may even decide vires questions at first instance — but always under the High Court's scrutiny.

Q2What is the "parent statute" exception?

A tribunal is a creature of the statute that establishes it. It cannot sit in judgment on the validity of that very statute. So a challenge to the vires of the Administrative Tribunals Act itself must be brought directly in the High Court. Every other question of vires within the tribunal's field must first be raised before the tribunal.

Q3Explain the treatment of section 5(6) of the Administrative Tribunals Act.

Section 5(6) permits the Chairman to authorise a single Member to hear specified classes of cases. It was attacked because a lone Administrative Member might decide questions of law. The Court upheld it by harmonious construction: where a question involving the interpretation of a statutory provision or rule in relation to the Constitution arises before a single Member, it must be referred to a Bench of at least two Members, one of whom is a Judicial Member. Strong

Q4What practical route does a service litigant follow after this decision?

Original application before the tribunal → writ petition under Articles 226/227 before a Division Bench of the High Court → special leave petition under Article 136. No direct High Court writ bypassing the tribunal; no direct SLP bypassing the High Court.

22Top 10 Exam Traps

  5. Saying a litigant may go straight to the High Court.

  7. Calling the reference Bench a Constitution Bench.
