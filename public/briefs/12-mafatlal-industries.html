Mafatlal Industries Ltd. v. Union of India

A manufacturer who has already recovered the duty from his customers cannot recover it again from the State. Nine judges on refunds of tax, unjust enrichment, and when a writ or suit will lie.

(1997) 5 SCC 536[1996] Supp. 10 SCR 5851997 (89) ELT 247 (SC)19 December 19969 Judges · majority of 5, with separate opinions

Read this first — learn it as three boxes

Every refund claim falls into one of three categories, and the category decides the forum, the limitation and the defence:

  • Unconstitutional levy — the charging provision itself is void. Claim lies outside the Act: suit or writ.
  • Illegal levy — the Act is valid but was misread or misapplied. Claim lies only under the Act (section 11B / section 27).
  • Mistake of law discovered from someone else's case — no claim.

Over all three sits one rule: the claimant must show he has not passed the burden on.

Statutory names have changed. The Central Excises and Salt Act, 1944 is now the Central Excise Act, 1944, and since 2017 most of this field is governed by the CGST Act, section 54, which carries the same unjust-enrichment bar. Strong The principles are applied by analogy.

On sources. No judgment file was uploaded. I worked from the Supreme Court's head-notes volume (which, in the part I could read, covers the majority only) and the settled reported position. §30 lists what I could not verify.

02If I Have Only 60 Seconds

Facts → Issue → Rule → Ratio → Holding → Provision → Significance

  1. Setting. Hundreds of assessees sought refund of excise and customs duty — some because a levy was struck down, some because a later decision showed they had overpaid.
  2. Old law. Kanhaiya Lal (1959): tax paid under mistake of law is refundable under section 72, Contract Act; whether the payer passed it on is irrelevant.
  3. 1991. Parliament amended section 11B (and section 27, Customs Act): refund only if the claimant has not passed on the incidence; otherwise the money goes to the Consumer Welfare Fund.
  4. Issue. Is that valid? Can the Act be bypassed by suit or writ? Is there an absolute right to refund under Article 265?
  5. Ratio. Refund is governed by restitution; no one may be unjustly enriched. No refund unless the burden was not passed on.
  6. Forum. Illegal levy → only under the Act. Unconstitutional levy → suit or writ, still subject to the passing-on rule.
  7. Holding. 1991 amendments valid. Kanhaiya Lal overruled on the relevant points.

03One-Line Ratio

The sentence to reproduce

The ratio in one line

A claim for refund of tax — whether under the statute, by suit or by writ — is a claim for restitution and succeeds only if the claimant pleads and proves that he has not passed on the burden to another; where the levy is merely illegal the claim lies only under the Act (section 11B / section 27), and where the levy is unconstitutional it lies outside the Act but remains subject to the same bar of unjust enrichment. Certain

04Facts

What actually happened

  • Indirect taxes are passed on. Excise and customs duty is ordinarily built into the price and borne by the buyer.
  • Before 1991. Following Kanhaiya Lal, manufacturers who succeeded in showing a levy was wrong obtained refunds through writ petitions and suits under section 72 of the Contract Act, though they had already collected the duty from customers. Some claims were made years later, after someone else won a case.
  • The 1991 Amendment (in force 20 September 1991 Strong) recast the refund provisions:
  • Section 11B — refund only on application within the prescribed time, and only if the claimant establishes that the incidence has not been passed on; otherwise the amount is credited to the Fund; clause (3) gives the section overriding effect over any judgment or other law.
  • Section 12A — invoices to show the duty element. Section 12B — presumption that the incidence has been passed on to the buyer. Section 12C — Consumer Welfare Fund.
  • Section 11D — duty collected from buyers must be paid over to the Government.
  • Corresponding provisions in the Customs Act — sections 27, 28C, 28D. Strong
  • The batch. Assessees challenged the amendments and pressed pending refund claims; the Revenue challenged refund orders. Because Kanhaiya Lal was by five Judges and later Benches had divided, the matters went to nine. Strong

05Procedural History

How it reached the Court

  1. Appeals from High Court orders allowing or refusing refund in writ petitions and suits.
  2. Writ petitions under Article 32 challenging the 1991 Amendment.
  3. Reference to a larger Bench in view of conflicting decisions and the five-judge decision in Kanhaiya Lal. Strong
  4. Judgment on 19 December 1996; the individual cases were then to be disposed of by appropriate Benches applying the propositions. Strong

08Ratio Decidendi

The propositions

1 — The three categories

2 — Illegal levy: the Act is exclusive

Where duty was collected by misinterpreting or misapplying the Act, rules or notifications, refund must be claimed under and in accordance with the Act, before the authorities it creates and within its limitation. No suit lies. The writ jurisdiction under Articles 226 and 32 cannot be taken away, but the court will exercise it having regard to the legislative intent — it will not grant what the Act forbids. Certain

3 — Unconstitutional levy: outside the Act

Where the provision under which duty was levied is struck down, the claim is not under the Act — the Act does not contemplate its own invalidity. It may be pursued by suit or by writ, as a claim for restitution; the period of limitation is that under the Limitation Act. Certain But a person who himself challenged the provision and failed cannot take advantage of a later declaration obtained by another on a different ground. Certain

4 — Another's case gives no cause of action

An assessee who paid without protest, or whose proceedings ended, cannot claim refund because a court later decided the point in another assessee's favour. He must fight his own case within time. "Discovery of mistake" from another's judgment does not extend limitation. Certain

5 — Unjust enrichment

In every category the claimant must allege and establish that he has not passed on the burden of the duty to another person. If he has, the real loser is the consumer; repayment to the manufacturer would be a windfall. Where the consumers cannot be identified, it is just that the amount be retained by the State, which represents the people — the doctrine of unjust enrichment applies against the assessee, not against the State. Certain

6 — Article 265 and section 72

Neither confers an automatic right to refund. Restitution is equitable; a person who has suffered no loss has nothing to be restored. Certain

7 — Validity and reach of the 1991 Amendment

Section 11B and the allied provisions are valid and do not offend Articles 14, 19(1)(g) or 265. The Central Excise Act is a self-contained code on levy, collection and refund. Amended section 11B applies to pending refund proceedings, but not where the refund order had become final before the amendment. The State cannot recover refunds already made where no proceedings were pending. Certain

09Obiter & Observations

What is not binding

  • Captive consumption. Whether the passing-on rule applies where the duty-paid goods are used by the claimant himself in manufacture was left open by the majority. Strong It was later answered in Union of India v. Solar Pesticides Pvt. Ltd. (2000) — the doctrine applies. Strong
  • Provisional assessments. Observations on refund consequent on finalisation of provisional assessment were later the subject of legislative change (proviso to Rule 9B; section 18, Customs Act) and further cases. Strong
  • The State's plea of financial chaos — the majority noted it but held it is not an answer to an assessee who has in fact borne the duty. Certain

10Judicial Reasoning

How the majority reasoned

  1. Nature of the claim. Refund is restitution — giving back what the payer has lost. If he has lost nothing, there is nothing to restore.
  2. Equity of section 72. English and Indian restitution law recognise defences where repayment would be inequitable.
  3. Article 265. Its command is against the State collecting without law. It is silent on to whom money should go when the person who paid is not the person who bore it.
  4. Who should keep the money. Between a manufacturer who has lost nothing and the State which holds for the public, the latter has the better claim; the Consumer Welfare Fund reflects this.
  5. Statutory exclusivity. A special Act creating a right or liability and a machinery for it excludes the general remedy (Dhulabhai). An illegal levy is still a levy "under the Act".
  6. Finality. Tax administration needs certainty; assessments cannot be reopened whenever the law is clarified in another's case.
  7. Constitutional courts. Their jurisdiction survives, but they do not sit to defeat a valid statute.

Dissenting and concurring opinions

  • Hansaria J. — agreed substantially with the majority, with reservations on a limited aspect. Strong

Trap

The official head-note text available to me set out the majority only. The descriptions above of the four other opinions are from standard accounts — tagged yellow. In an answer, attribute the propositions to "the majority per B.P. Jeevan Reddy J." and name the dissenter as S.C. Sen J.

14Final Holding

The operative result

  • Section 11B (before and after 1991) and the allied provisions — constitutionally valid. Certain
  • All refund claims for illegal levy — only under the Act; suits barred. Certain
  • Claims on unconstitutional levy — by suit or writ, subject to proof that the burden was not passed on. Certain
  • No claim on the basis of a decision in another's case. Certain
  • Kanhaiya Lal and the cases following it overruled to the extent indicated. Certain

16AOR-Specific Importance

Why an AOR candidate must own this case

  • Maintainability of tax writs. The first objection to any refund petition under Article 226 or 32: is this an "illegal levy" claim that must go under the statute?
  • Pleading. A refund petition must contain a specific averment, with supporting material (cost sheets, invoices, chartered accountant's certificate), that the incidence was not passed on.
  • Limitation. Statutory period under section 11B / section 27 / section 54 for illegal levy; Limitation Act for unconstitutional levy.
  • Advising clients. Pay under protest and litigate your own case — a favourable ruling in someone else's will not revive a time-barred claim.
  • Restitution generally. Cited well beyond tax — wherever a party seeks repayment of money it has already recouped.
  • Links. Kailash Nath Associates (34) on compensation and loss; CCI v. Bharti Airtel (51) on statutory forum.

172-Mark Questions

Answer in two or three lines

Q1What is the doctrine of unjust enrichment as applied in Mafatlal?

No person may retain a benefit at another's expense; a taxpayer who has passed the duty on to buyers cannot obtain refund, since he has suffered no loss.

Q2Name the three categories of refund claim.

Unconstitutional levy; illegal levy (misinterpretation or misapplication); mistake of law discovered through another's case.

Q3Does a suit lie for refund of duty collected by misinterpreting the Act?

No. The claim lies only under section 11B (or section 27, Customs Act).

Q4Which earlier decision was overruled?

Sales Tax Officer, Banaras v. Kanhaiya Lal Mukundlal Saraf (1959), to the extent indicated.

Q5Does Article 265 confer an absolute right to refund?

No.

Q6Bench and majority author?

Nine Judges; B.P. Jeevan Reddy J. for five.

185-Mark Questions

Answer in about 150 words

Q1Distinguish between an "unconstitutional levy" and an "illegal levy" and state the consequence.

An unconstitutional levy is one where the charging provision itself is struck down as beyond legislative competence or violative of the Constitution. An illegal levy is one where the provision is valid but duty was collected by misreading or misapplying the Act, rules or a notification. In the first case the claim arises outside the Act and may be enforced by suit or writ as restitution, within the limitation in the Limitation Act. In the second the levy is still "under the Act", and refund can be sought only through section 11B, before the departmental authorities and within its time limit; a civil suit is barred and the writ court will respect the statutory scheme. In both, the claimant must prove he did not pass on the burden.

Q2Why does the doctrine of unjust enrichment not apply against the State?

Because the State does not hold the money for its own benefit. Tax collected is spent for the public. When the persons who actually bore the duty — innumerable consumers — cannot be traced, retention by the State is the nearest approach to restoring it to them. A manufacturer who has recouped the duty has no better claim.

Q3Can an assessee claim refund on the basis of a judgment in another assessee's case?

No. If his own assessment has become final, or he paid without protest and did not challenge it within time, he cannot reopen it because the law was later declared differently in another's case. He cannot invoke section 17(1)(c) of the Limitation Act on the ground that he "discovered" his mistake from that judgment.

Q4What did the 1991 Amendment change, and was it upheld?

It conditioned refund on proof that the incidence was not passed on, created a presumption of passing-on, required duty to be shown on invoices, set up the Consumer Welfare Fund, and gave section 11B overriding effect. All upheld against challenge under Articles 14, 19(1)(g) and 265.

22Top 10 Exam Traps

Ten ways to lose marks

  1. Saying writ jurisdiction is barred.
  2. Saying a suit lies for illegal levy.
  3. Treating unconstitutional levy as free of the passing-on test.
  4. Calling the judgment unanimous.
  5. Saying Article 265 gives an automatic refund.
  6. Putting the burden of proof on the Revenue.
  7. Forgetting that Kanhaiya Lal was overruled.
  8. Saying the State may be unjustly enriched — the majority rejected the label.
  9. Allowing refund on the strength of another's case.
  10. Citing section 11B for customs — that is section 27.
